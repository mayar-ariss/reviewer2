# Spin: Boutron and Ravaud on misrepresenting results

Load this file when the user wants a draft checked for spin: reporting that makes the results look better, clearer or more important than they are, especially in the abstract and the conclusions. **Scope: health research only.** Boutron and Ravaud's review is limited to biomedicine; do not apply this file to papers in other fields.

**Every rule below is Boutron and Ravaud's.** Their review is open access and was read in full; rules cite it by section. Where they give no rule, this file says so rather than supplying one. Nothing here is a general writing preference.

*Sources and how they are cited are listed at the end of this file.*

## How to apply this file

*Procedure, not a rule of Boutron and Ravaud.*

1. **Read the Methods and Results first, then the abstract and the conclusions,** and compare them (rule 7).
2. **Check the reporting of methods** against the protocol or registration, where the author can supply it (rule 2).
3. **Check the reporting of results** for selection and misleading presentation (rule 3).
4. **Check the interpretation** against the results (rule 4) and the rhetoric (rule 5).
5. **Report each instance to the author as a question,** citing the rule. Spin may be unintentional (rule 1); do not accuse the author of intent. Do not rewrite the results.
6. **Run the checklist** at the end.

## Rules

### 1. Know what spin is, whatever the intent

*Source: B&R 2018, "Definition of the Concept of Spin."*

Spin is reporting that fails to faithfully reflect the nature and range of the findings and that could affect the impression the results make on readers: a way to distort science reporting without actually lying. It may be unconscious and unintentional. Choices about which analyses to report, how to report and interpret them, and what rhetoric to use can be legitimate in one context and create an inaccurate impression in another. It is almost impossible to tell whether spin comes from misunderstanding, habit or a wish to mislead, but when it occurs it often favours the author's interests.

- Judge the text, not the author: flag what could mislead a reader, and say why.

### 2. Check that the methods are reported as they were carried out

*Source: B&R 2018, "Misreporting the Methods."*

Methods can be misreported by changing objectives, reporting post hoc hypotheses as if they were prespecified, switching outcomes or analyses, or masking deviations from the protocol. Boutron and Ravaud also describe "beautification": reporting methods as if they met the highest standards when they did not, such as calling a study double-blind when the blinding is not credible, reporting an intention-to-treat analysis while excluding some patients, calling a study randomized when it was not, or claiming adherence to reporting guidelines that the report does not meet.

- Ask the author for the protocol, registration or analysis plan, and compare the stated objectives, outcomes and analyses with it.
- Check that each methodological label (*randomized*, *double-blind*, *intention-to-treat*) is supported by what the Methods describe.

### 3. Check that results are reported completely and displayed fairly

*Source: B&R 2018, "Misreporting Results."*

Misreporting results is incomplete or inadequate reporting that could mislead the reader. It includes:

- **Selective reporting** of outcomes and analyses according to their results. Practices that can lead to selecting significant results include interim analyses to decide whether to stop, post hoc exclusion of outliers, combining or splitting groups, adjusting covariates, subgroup analyses and choosing thresholds for dichotomizing continuous outcomes.
- **Ignoring or understating** results that contradict the hypothesis, such as non-significant outcomes or significant harms.
- **Misleading metrics and figures:** for example, reporting relative rather than absolute risk reduction when baseline risk is low, odds ratios read as risk ratios when baseline risk is high, a broken y-axis, missing confidence intervals, axes scaled to the results, or survival curves extended without showing uncertainty. Image processing can also shape the impression a figure gives.

- Check that every prespecified outcome is reported, including non-significant ones and harms.
- Check each figure for uncertainty, axis breaks and scaling, and each effect for the metric used.

### 4. Check that the interpretation follows from the results

*Source: B&R 2018, "Misinterpretation."*

Misinterpretation is interpretation not consistent with the study's actual results. Boutron and Ravaud list as misleading: relying on a within-group comparison; ignoring regression to the mean and confounding; inappropriately positing causality; drawing an inappropriate inference from a composite outcome; reporting a P value as if it measured the size of an effect; reading non-significant results as showing equivalence or safety without the power to do so; and interpreting statistically significant but small effects without caution. Hypothesizing after the results are known (HARKing) and justifying after the results are known (JARKing) are also inappropriate. Authors may extrapolate beyond the data to a larger population, setting or intervention, from animals to humans, or to recommendations for practice.

- For each interpretive claim, find the result it rests on, and check it against this list.
- Flag causal wording for observational results, and recommendations that go beyond the population and setting studied.

### 5. Check the rhetoric

*Source: B&R 2018, "Other Types of Spin."*

Rhetoric, language designed to persuade or impress, can exaggerate the importance of the topic, unfairly dismiss previous work, or push a point of view. Boutron and Ravaud describe a typical pattern: the disease is a "critical public health priority", previous work had "inconsistent results" or "methodologic flaws", and this is "the first study" giving "strong" evidence or "a clear answer". They report an 880% increase in positive words in PubMed abstracts between 1974 and 2014. References, too, can be chosen to fit the message, with authors citing studies whose conclusions match their own.

- Flag claims of priority (*first*, *novel*), strength (*strong*, *clear*, *robust*) and dismissals of earlier work, and ask what supports each.
- Check whether the cited literature includes studies with contrary findings.

### 6. Look hardest at the abstract's conclusions

*Source: B&R 2018, "Prevalence of Some Forms of Spin in Published Reports"; "Impact of Spin."*

In randomized trials with non-significant primary outcomes, spin was most common in the abstract's conclusions, found in more than half of reports. In observational studies evaluating an intervention, spin appeared in the abstract's conclusions of more than 80% of reports, most often as causal language. In a randomized study, researchers who read abstracts with spin were more likely to judge the treatment beneficial, and spin in abstracts was linked to spin in press releases and news.

- Check the abstract's conclusions first and most carefully, sentence by sentence, against the Results.

### 7. Check that the conclusions match the results

*Source: B&R 2018, "Editors, Peer-Review, and Postpublication Monitoring/Feedback"; "Reporting Guidelines and New Processes of Reporting."*

Peer reviewers and editors should determine whether the conclusions match the results, but in one systematic assessment reviewers failed to identify spin in the abstract's conclusions in 76% of reports, and only two-thirds of the spin they did identify was fully removed; some reviewers asked for spin to be added. Boutron and Ravaud note initiatives that reduce spin, such as a journal requiring a limitation to be reported in the abstract, and the American Statistical Association's statement on the proper use of P values.

- For each conclusion in the abstract and the Discussion, quote the result that supports it. Flag conclusions with no supporting result, or stronger than the result.
- Check whether the abstract states a limitation.

## Where Boutron and Ravaud give no rule

The review covers biomedicine; its prevalence figures come from specific bodies of biomedical research. Boutron and Ravaud note that no guideline on avoiding spin was available, and that reporting guidelines say what to report but not how to interpret it. They give no rule for deciding intent (rule 1). This file adds nothing where they are silent.

## Example

*An illustration of this file's procedure, not Boutron and Ravaud's.* An abstract concludes: "Treatment X is a promising and effective option for patients," while the Results report no significant difference in the primary outcome and a significant improvement in one secondary outcome.

- **Results (rule 3):** the non-significant primary outcome must be reported in the abstract, not only the significant secondary one.
- **Interpretation (rules 4, 7):** "effective" is not supported by the primary result. Ask the author to state the primary result and to frame the secondary result as exploratory, if it was not prespecified as a key outcome.
- **Rhetoric (rule 5):** "promising" signals benefit the primary result does not show.

## Validation checklist

- [ ] The text was judged, not the author: what could mislead a reader was flagged, with the reason (rule 1)
- [ ] The Methods and Results were read before the abstract and conclusions (procedure)
- [ ] Stated objectives, outcomes and analyses were compared with the protocol or registration where available (rule 2)
- [ ] Methodological labels are supported by the Methods (rule 2)
- [ ] All prespecified outcomes, including non-significant ones and harms, are reported (rule 3)
- [ ] Figures and effect metrics were checked for misleading presentation (rule 3)
- [ ] Each interpretation was traced to a result and checked against the list of misinterpretations (rule 4)
- [ ] Rhetorical claims of priority, strength and dismissal were flagged (rule 5)
- [ ] The abstract's conclusions were checked sentence by sentence against the Results (rules 6–7)
- [ ] Instances were raised as questions; no intent was attributed and no result was rewritten

# Cohesion: Halliday and Hasan

Load this file when the user wants a draft checked for how its sentences are tied together: what its pronouns, possessives, demonstratives (*this*, *these*, *that*, *the*) and comparatives (*same*, *other*, *such*, *more*) point to; whether its linking words (*however*, *therefore*, *then*) signal the relation that actually holds; whether its vocabulary keeps track of the same things; and whether what a substitute stands for, or what has been left out, can be recovered. It applies to any part of a research article, thesis, dissertation or proposal.

**Every rule below is Halliday and Hasan's.** Their book was not available to check, so each rule cites it as reported in a named open source. Where they give no rule, this file says so rather than supplying one. Nothing here is a general writing preference.

Halliday and Hasan define cohesion as non-structural text-forming relations (p. 7, as quoted by Crane): the ties of meaning between one element and another that it presupposes (p. 4, as reported by Crane). Rules 1–4 cover reference, rule 5 conjunction, rule 6 lexical cohesion and rule 7 substitution and ellipsis.

*Sources and how they are cited are listed at the end of this file.*

## How to apply this file

*Procedure, not a rule of Halliday and Hasan.*

1. **Mark every reference item** in the passage: personal (*it*, *they*, *their*, *its*), demonstrative (*this*, *these*, *that*, *those*, *the*, *here*, *there*) and comparative (*same*, *similar*, *other*, *such*, *more*, *less*) (rule 2).
2. **For each, find what it points to** in the text, and say whether a reader can recover it with certainty (rules 3–4).
3. **Check that the referent makes sense where the item stands** (rule 4).
4. **Mark every conjunction** linking sentences or clauses, name the relation it signals, and check that relation against what the sentences say (rule 5).
5. **Mark the words that name the paper's key objects and quantities**, and check that each is named consistently (rule 6).
6. **Mark substitutes and ellipses** (*one*, *do so*, *the same*, a noun or verb left unsaid), and check that the reader can supply what they stand for (rule 7).
7. **Propose revisions citing the rule.** Do not guess the intended referent, relation or term; where it cannot be recovered, ask the author.
8. **Run the checklist** at the end.

## Rules

### 1. Treat a reference item as an instruction to look elsewhere

*Source: H&H 1976, pp. 31–32 (Ahmed); JBM.*

Reference items cannot be interpreted in their own right; they point to something else for their interpretation. What characterizes reference, in Halliday and Hasan's account, is the specific nature of the information the reader is signalled to retrieve (pp. 31–32, as reported by Ahmed). Cohesion through reference is the presupposition of something that has gone before (JBM).

- Read each reference item as a promise that the referent can be found.

### 2. Know the three kinds of reference

*Source: H&H 1976, pp. 37–39, 51, 57, 71, 78 (Ahmed; Nguyen; Sandulescu; Crane).*

Halliday and Hasan distinguish personal reference, through the category of person (*he*, *it*, *they*, *their*); demonstrative reference, through a scale of proximity (*this*, *that*, *the*, *here*, *there*); and comparative reference, through identity or similarity (*same*, *other*, *such*, *more*) (Ahmed, citing p. 51; Nguyen, citing pp. 38–39; Sandulescu). The definite article *the* is treated as demonstrative reference (Sandulescu). Demonstrative reference is a form of verbal pointing on a scale of proximity (p. 57); *the* is semantically selective, identifying a particular referent (p. 71); and comparison is itself a referential property (p. 78) (as quoted by Crane).

- Include *the* and comparatives in the check, not only pronouns.

### 3. Point inside the text, and usually backward

*Source: H&H 1976, pp. 32–33, 51 (Ahmed; Cerban; Crane).*

Reference is either exophoric, pointing to the situation outside the text, or endophoric, pointing within it; endophoric reference is anaphoric when it points back to what has been said and cataphoric when it points forward (pp. 32–33, as reported by Ahmed and Cerban). Anaphoric reference ties a sentence to a referent already introduced (Cerban): it provides a link with a preceding portion of the text, which makes it the kind most relevant to cohesion (p. 51, as quoted by Crane). Exophoric reference depends on knowledge shared between writer and reader (Nguyen).

- In a research text, check that each reference item points to something in the text, and that the reader can find it there.
- Where an item points outside the text, ask whether the reader can be expected to share what it points to.

### 4. Make sure the referent can be recovered, and fits

*Source: H&H 1976, as reported by Ahmed and JBM.*

Whatever the kind of reference, there is a presupposition that must be satisfied: the thing referred to has to be identifiable (Ahmed). If the identity of a participant cannot be recovered, participant identification breaks down, and with it the cohesion of the text (JBM).

- For each reference item, name the referent a reader would recover. If there are two candidates, or none, flag it.
- If the grammatical candidate does not make sense in the clause (it cannot have, do or be what the clause says), the reader cannot recover the intended referent. Flag it, and ask the author what was meant.

### 5. Make each conjunction signal the relation that holds

*Source: H&H 1976, pp. 227, 250 (Crane). Application to checking is this skill's.*

Halliday and Hasan group conjunction into four types: additive (*and*, *also*, *furthermore*; negatively *nor*), adversative, signalling something contrary to expectation (*yet*, *but*, *though*; p. 250), causal, expressing result, reason or purpose (*so*, *because*, *as a result*), and temporal, linking by sequence or time (*then*, *next*, *until then*) (Crane). Conjunctive relations are not tied to any particular sequence in the expression (p. 227, as quoted by Crane): the conjunction states a relation of meaning, which the reader then looks for.

- For each conjunction, name its type and check that the relation holds between what the sentences say. Flag an adversative with no expectation to contradict, or a causal conjunction where the text gives no cause.
- Whether a link should be stated at all is a different question: see `rules/sentences.md`, rule 9.

### 6. Keep track of the same thing with the same word, or a recognizably related one

*Source: H&H 1976, pp. 274, 286 (Crane). Application to research writing is this skill's.*

Lexical cohesion is the cohesive effect achieved by the selection of vocabulary (p. 274, as quoted by Crane). Its two categories are reiteration, in which an item is repeated directly or through a synonym, a superordinate or a general word, and collocation, between items that share the same lexical environment (p. 286, as quoted by Crane).

- For each key object or quantity, list the words used for it. Where a draft moves between terms (*participants*, *respondents*, *the sample*), ask whether they name the same thing; in a research text a new term may be read as a new referent.
- Where a general word (*thing*, *issue*, *aspect*, *factor*) stands for something named earlier, check that the reader can tell which.

### 7. Make what a substitute stands for, or what is left out, recoverable

*Source: H&H 1976, pp. 4, 125–126, 142 (Crane). Application to research writing is this skill's.*

Cohesion links a presupposing element to a presupposed one (p. 4, as reported by Crane). Halliday and Hasan treat *do* as a verbal substitute even when it occurs with *it* or *that* (pp. 125–126, as reported by Crane), and treat ellipsis as substitution by zero (p. 142, as quoted by Crane): the reader must supply from the text what has been left unsaid.

- For each substitute (*one*, *ones*, *do*, *do so*, *so*, *the same*), name what it stands for. If there are two candidates, or none, flag it.
- For each ellipsis (a noun, verb or clause left out), check that the reader can supply exactly one item from the preceding text; if not, ask the author to state it.

## Where Halliday and Hasan give no rule

*Cohesion in English* describes how texts hang together; it is not a style guide. It gives no limit on distance between a reference item and its referent, no rule against starting a sentence with an unattended *this* (that discussion belongs to other authors), no rule on how often to repeat a term, and no preference among conjunctions of the same type. The sources used here report Halliday and Hasan's treatment of substitution and ellipsis only in part, so rule 7 is brief. Because the book itself was not available, this file covers only the points the reporting sources give. This file adds nothing where Halliday and Hasan are silent.

## Example

*An illustration of this file's procedure, not Halliday and Hasan's.* Draft sentence: "Recent crises show how much rests on their supply chains."

- **Reference item (rule 2):** *their* is personal reference, a possessive that presupposes a plural referent.
- **Referent (rules 3–4):** the only candidate in the sentence is *recent crises*. Crises do not have supply chains, so the referent the grammar points to does not fit the clause, and the one the author presumably meant (firms, sectors or countries) is not in the text. Ask the author what *their* refers to, and name it.

A second draft passage, from an education study: "The survey was sent to all 40 schools in March. However, it contained 42 items. The sample then shrank."

- **Conjunction (rule 5):** *however* is adversative, but nothing in the first sentence sets up an expectation the second contradicts. Ask the author what the contrast is, or whether a different relation was meant.
- **Lexical cohesion (rule 6):** *the sample* may be the schools, the teachers or the pupils who answered. Ask which is meant, and keep one term for it.

## Validation checklist

- [ ] Each reference item was treated as an instruction to look elsewhere, and followed (rule 1)
- [ ] Every personal, demonstrative and comparative reference item was marked (rule 2)
- [ ] Each points to something the reader can find in the text, or that the reader can be expected to share (rule 3)
- [ ] Each has exactly one recoverable referent (rule 4)
- [ ] Each referent makes sense in the clause where the item stands (rule 4)
- [ ] Each conjunction signals a relation that holds between the sentences it links (rule 5)
- [ ] Each key object and quantity is named consistently, and general words can be traced (rule 6)
- [ ] Each substitute and each ellipsis can be resolved to exactly one item in the text (rule 7)
- [ ] No referent, relation or term was guessed; unclear ones were raised with the author

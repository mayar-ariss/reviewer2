# Claims: Hyland on stance and metadiscourse

Load this file when the user wants the claims in a draft checked: how strongly each is stated, where it comes from, and how the writer stands toward it and toward the reader. It applies to any part of a research article, thesis, dissertation or proposal, and can follow another rules file (for example, after the moves of an introduction have been checked).

**Every rule below is Hyland's.** Each names its source by work and page. Where Hyland gives no rule, this file says so rather than supplying one. Nothing here is a general writing preference.

## How to apply this file

*Procedure, not a Hyland rule.* Hyland identifies these features by what they do in context, not by word lists (rule 2), so the agent reads each sentence rather than searching for words:

1. **Ask for the field and the genre**, or read them from the draft. Rules 6–8 depend on field.
2. **Find the claims.** Mark each sentence that states a finding, an interpretation or an evaluation.
3. **For each claim, ask four questions:** Where does it come from (rule 3)? How strongly is it stated, and does the evidence support that strength (rules 4–5)? What attitude does it express, and whose (rules 7–8)? If it reports others' work, what does the reporting verb commit the writer to (rule 9)?
4. **Propose changes citing the rule.** Keep the author's claims, data and citations. Do not supply evidence, sources or findings; where a claim needs support the draft does not have, ask the author for it.
5. **Run the checklist** at the end.

## The model

Hyland's interpersonal model divides metadiscourse into two kinds of resource (Hyland 2010, p. 128; Hyland 2017 (MS), p. 7):

| Kind | Category | What it does | Examples |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Interactive:** guides the reader through the text | Transitions | Mark how main clauses relate | *in addition, but, thus* |
| | Frame markers | Mark discourse acts, sequences or stages | *finally, to conclude, my purpose is* |
| | Endophoric markers | Point to other parts of the text | *noted above, see Fig., in section 2* |
| | **Evidentials** | Point to the source of information in other texts | *according to X, (Y, 1990), Z states* |
| | Code glosses | Help the reader grasp a meaning | *namely, e.g., such as, in other words* |
| **Interactional:** involves the reader in the argument | **Hedges** | Hold back the writer's full commitment to a proposition | *might, perhaps, possible, about* |
| | **Boosters** | Stress the force of a proposition or the writer's certainty | *in fact, definitely, it is clear that* |
| | **Attitude markers** | Express the writer's attitude to a proposition | *unfortunately, I agree, surprisingly* |
| | Engagement markers | Address the reader or build a relationship with them | *consider, note that, you can see that* |
| | **Self-mentions** | Refer explicitly to the author(s) | *I, we, my, our* |

In Hyland's stance framework, as Hyland and Jiang summarize it, stance is the writer-oriented side of interaction, complementing engagement, and has three components: **evidentiality** (commitment to the reliability of statements, through hedges and boosters), **affect** (attitude, through attitude markers) and **presence** (first-person reference) (Hyland & Jiang 2023 (MS), pp. 4–5). The categories in bold are the ones this file checks.

## Rules

### 1. Write each claim for a reader who can reject it

*Source: Hyland 2010, p. 127; Hyland 2005, pp. 99–100, 111.*

Hyland treats writing as social engagement: writers project themselves into their texts and negotiate with readers (2010, p. 127). Hedges are part of how claims gain readers' acceptance, so they belong to the negotiation of knowledge (2005, pp. 99–100). Whether a claim persuades depends in part on readers accepting that the writer has managed its commitment appropriately, and what counts as appropriate depends on the discipline's assumptions and criteria for justification (2005, p. 111).

- Read each claim as a reader in the field would: what would make them accept or reject it?

### 2. Identify hedges, boosters and the rest by what they do in the sentence

*Source: Hyland 2017 (MS), pp. 3–5; Hyland 2010, p. 133.*

Metadiscourse is defined by function, not form: the same word can do different jobs, so each instance has to be checked in context (2017 (MS), pp. 4–5). It is distinguished from propositional content, the material about the world that is affirmed, doubted or qualified (2017 (MS), p. 3). Hyland separates epistemic uses of modals such as *may*, which comment on the claim and are metadiscourse, from deontic uses, which refer to the world (2010, p. 133). Word lists can overcount; reading each occurrence in context matters more than counting (2017 (MS), p. 5).

- *may* in "the method may fail at high flow" is a hedge; *may* in "participants may withdraw at any time" is not.
- Do not judge a draft by counting markers.

### 3. Show where each claim comes from

*Source: Hyland 2010, pp. 128, 134; Hyland 2005, pp. 101–102.*

Evidentials point to the source of a claim in other texts (2010, p. 128). Citing others both justifies a claim and displays the writer as credible; in Hyland's dissertation corpus, doctoral writers used over four times as many intertextual references as masters writers (2010, p. 134).

Where a claim is the writer's own, Hyland describes two ways of presenting it. The writer can take it on, for example with a verb of cognition (*we suggest*, *I believe*), which presents the claim as a provisional position (2005, p. 102; see rule 5). Or the writer can step back from it, with an impersonal subject such as dummy *it* (*it seems that*) or an abstract rhetor, an inanimate subject that does the suggesting (*thermodynamic data suggest*) (2005, pp. 101–102).

- For each claim, decide: does it come from other work (it needs an evidential), from the writer's own data or reasoning (it needs to be presented as such), or from neither?
- An evaluative claim with no source and no writer standing behind it is the case to raise with the author. Ask for the source or the evidence; do not supply one.

### 4. State each claim with the certainty the evidence supports

*Source: Hyland 2010, pp. 128, 132, 137; Hyland 2005, pp. 100–101.*

Hedges withhold the writer's full commitment to a proposition; boosters stress its force or the writer's certainty (2010, p. 128). Hedges let writers present a claim with the reliability it actually has: writers say that X may cause Y rather than that X causes Y, marking the claim as an inference rather than a certainty (2005, pp. 100–101). In the dissertations Hyland studied, hedges were the most frequent interactional feature, about 41% of all interactional uses, with *may*, *could* and *would* among the commonest forms (2010, p. 132). He describes metadiscourse as helping writers manage the risks of presenting appropriate opinions and degrees of certainty (2010, p. 137).

- Match each claim's strength to its evidence: a result the data show directly can be stated firmly; an interpretation, explanation or generalization beyond the data is hedged.
- Treat strong verbs as boosters when they claim more than the evidence shows (see rule 9 for reporting verbs).

### 5. Hedge for accuracy, for protection and for the reader

*Source: Hyland 2005, pp. 100–102.*

Hyland gives three reasons for hedging (2005, pp. 100–102):

- **Accuracy:** to state claims with the reliability they actually have (pp. 100–101).
- **Protecting the writer:** to avoid overstatement and the cost of being proved wrong, often by distancing the writer from a speculative claim with an impersonal structure (pp. 101–102).
- **The reader:** to soften assertions that could threaten readers. Categorical claims leave no room for dialogue; hedges with verbs of cognition present claims as provisional and treat readers as intelligent colleagues (p. 102).

A hedge therefore looks three ways, toward the proposition, the writer and the reader (p. 102).

- When proposing a hedge, say which of the three it serves.

### 6. Calibrate to the field: soft fields hedge more than hard ones

*Source: Hyland 2005, pp. 103–109; Hyland 2010, pp. 137–138.*

Across 56 research articles in eight disciplines, hedges averaged 14.6 per 1,000 words, about 85 per paper (2005, p. 104). The rate ranged from 20.0 per 1,000 words in marketing and 18.5 in philosophy to 9.6 in physics and 8.2 in electrical engineering; over 70% of the hedges occurred in the humanities and social sciences (2005, Table 1, p. 106). Hyland explains the difference by knowledge and readership. In hard fields, knowledge is cumulative and readers share background, so findings are presented more impersonally and hedged less. Soft fields are more interpretive, and their readers more varied and less likely to share assumptions, so writers hedge more (2005, pp. 106–108). Hard and soft fields also prefer different hedging verbs: discourse verbs such as *indicate*, *suggest* and *imply* in the sciences, verbs of cognition such as *think*, *believe* and *suspect* in the soft fields (2005, pp. 108–109).

Genre matters too: hedges were nearly three times as frequent in research articles as in textbooks (2005, p. 103). In the dissertations, social science writers used over 60% more hedges than natural science writers (2010, p. 137).

- Compare the draft's hedging with recent papers in the target journal, not with an average across fields.
- Prefer the hedging verbs the field uses.

### 7. Make evaluations explicit and attributable

*Source: Hyland 2010, pp. 128, 138; Hyland & Jiang 2023 (MS), pp. 14, 16.*

Attitude markers express the writer's attitude to a proposition (2010, p. 128): an affective rather than an epistemic judgement, such as importance, surprise or agreement (Hyland & Jiang 2023 (MS), p. 14). Fields differ widely: in the dissertation corpus, biology writers used about a tenth of the stance markers that applied linguists used (2010, p. 138). In electrical engineering articles, attitude markers mainly signal significance rather than emotion (Hyland & Jiang 2023 (MS), p. 16).

- When a sentence evaluates (*important*, *crucial*, *much rests on*), check that the evaluation is the writer's, stated as such, and supported.

### 8. Use self-mention as the field does

*Source: Hyland 2010, pp. 128, 135–137; Hyland 2017 (MS), p. 19.*

Self-mentions refer explicitly to the author or authors (2010, p. 128). Doctoral dissertations contained about four times as many as masters theses, and masters writers were often unsure about using *I* or avoided it (2010, pp. 135–136). Rates varied by field, from 50.0 per 10,000 words in applied linguistics to 5.7 in biology (2010, p. 137). Hyland reports that undergraduates are far more reluctant to use self-mention than professional writers (2017 (MS), p. 19, citing Hyland 2012).

- Where the field uses first person, a claim the writer owns can say so (*we argue*, *our results suggest*).
- Where it does not, keep the impersonal forms of rule 3.

### 9. Choose reporting verbs for the commitment they carry

*Source: Hyland & Jiang 2023 (MS), pp. 4, 6, 9–10, 17.*

Reporting verbs signal the writer's stance toward cited work (Hyland & Jiang 2023 (MS), p. 4):

| Type | Signals | Examples |
|---|---|---|
| Factive | The writer accepts the cited claim | *demonstrate, confirm* |
| Counter-factive | The writer disagrees | *fail, overlook* |
| Non-factive | No clear attitude from the writer | *find, identify* |

Verbs such as *demonstrate*, *prove* and *show* commit the writer to the claim; *suggest*, *indicate* and *imply* leave room for the writer to withhold commitment (p. 4). Non-factive verbs can also attribute a stance to the cited author: positive (*argue*, *advocate*), neutral (*address*, *comment*), tentative (*suggest*, *believe*) or critical (*refute*, *object*) (p. 6). In their diachronic corpus, critical verbs have almost disappeared; writers who criticise tend to point to a gap in the literature rather than at a named author, and withholding stance toward colleagues' work has become a judicious strategy (pp. 9–10, 17).

- *show* makes the writer vouch for the claim. Use it only where the writer would.
- Where the draft disagrees with prior work, note that writers in Hyland and Jiang's corpus now usually state the gap rather than criticise the author (pp. 9–10), and let the author choose.

## Where Hyland gives no rule

The sources cited here describe practice; they do not prescribe it. Hyland gives no target number of hedges, boosters or self-mentions; his figures are averages for a corpus and a field, not quotas (rule 6). He gives no general rule for calibrating claims beyond matching commitment to evidence and to the field (rules 1, 4, 6). The sources read for this file do not discuss the problems of excessive hedging. The engagement categories of the *Discourse Studies* model (questions, directives, asides, appeals to shared knowledge) are not covered, because that article was not available to check. This file adds nothing where Hyland is silent.

## Example

*An illustration of this file's procedure, not Hyland's.* Draft sentence: "Extreme events show how much rests on their hydraulics."

- **Source (rule 3):** no evidential, and no writer stands behind the claim; the inanimate subject *extreme events* is not evidence in the way *the data* would be. Ask the author which events, and which study or result supports the claim.
- **Commitment (rules 4, 9):** *show* is factive: the sentence vouches for a claim it gives no grounds for.
- **Attitude (rule 7):** *how much rests on* is an evaluation of importance with no stated magnitude, holder or support.
- **Revision:** do not invent the content. Ask the author for the event, the hydraulic property, the result and the citation, then state the claim with the commitment that evidence supports.
- **Not covered here:** how the sentence is built (whose story it tells, where the action and the emphasis fall) is the subject of `rules/sentences.md`. That *their* refers to events, which have no hydraulics, is a failure of reference (see `rules/reference.md`); that *how much* states no quantity is for `rules/vagueness.md`.

## Validation checklist

- [ ] Features were identified by their function in each sentence, not by word lists (rule 2)
- [ ] Every claim has a source: an evidential, the writer's own data or reasoning, or a flag to the author (rule 3)
- [ ] Each claim's certainty matches its evidence; interpretations and generalizations are hedged (rules 4–5)
- [ ] Hedging density and hedging verbs match the field and the target journal (rule 6)
- [ ] Evaluations are explicit, attributable and supported (rule 7)
- [ ] Self-mention follows the field's practice (rule 8)
- [ ] Reporting verbs commit the writer only where the writer would commit (rule 9)
- [ ] No evidence, source or finding was invented; what the draft lacks was requested from the author

# Argument: Toulmin's model

Load this file when the user wants the argument of a draft checked: whether each main claim rests on stated data, whether the step from data to claim is justified, and whether the claim's strength and exceptions are stated. It applies to any part of a paper that argues, especially the Discussion, the conclusion and the introduction's case for the study.

**Every rule below is Toulmin's.** *The Uses of Argument* was not available to check, so rules cite it as quoted and explained in named open sources. Where Toulmin gives no rule, this file says so rather than supplying one. Nothing here is a general writing preference.

*Sources and how they are cited are listed at the end of this file.*

## How to apply this file

*Procedure, not a rule of Toulmin's.*

1. **List the main claims** of the passage: the conclusions the author wants the reader to accept (rule 1).
2. **For each claim, lay out the scheme:** data, warrant, backing, qualifier, rebuttal (see the model). Show the user the laid-out arguments.
3. **Mark the missing elements** and check each against rules 2–5. Then check the paper's figures and tables against the claims (rule 6).
4. **Propose revisions citing the rule.** Do not supply data, warrants, backing or exceptions; ask the author for them.
5. **Run the checklist** at the end.

## The model

Toulmin's scheme, with his example (Verheij, pp. 1–3):

| Element | What it is | Toulmin's example |
|---|---|---|
| **Claim (C)** | The conclusion whose merits are to be established | Harry is a British subject |
| **Data (D)** | The facts appealed to in support of the claim | Harry was born in Bermuda |
| **Warrant (W)** | A general, hypothetical statement that acts as a bridge from data to claim: an inference licence | A man born in Bermuda will generally be a British subject |
| **Backing (B)** | Support for the warrant, needed when the warrant's authority is challenged | The statutes and legal provisions on nationality |
| **Qualifier (Q)** | The degree of force the data give the claim by way of the warrant | *presumably* |
| **Rebuttal (R)** | Conditions of exception, which would set the warrant aside | Unless both his parents were aliens, or he has become a naturalized American |

## Rules

### 1. State each main claim and the data it rests on

*Source: Toulmin 1958, p. 97 (Verheij, p. 3).*

Toulmin distinguishes the claim or conclusion whose merits are to be established from the data appealed to as its foundation (p. 97, as quoted by Verheij, p. 3).

- For each main claim, name the data in the paper (results, cited findings) that support it. Flag claims with no data.

### 2. Make the warrant explicit where readers might not supply it

*Source: Toulmin 1958, p. 98 (Verheij, pp. 4, 7).*

Warrants are general, hypothetical statements that act as bridges from data to claim (p. 98, as quoted by Verheij, p. 7). In ordinary argument the link from data to claim is usually left unstated (Verheij, p. 4).

- For each claim, write out the warrant that would license the step from its data. If the warrant is one a reader in the field might doubt, ask the author to state it.

### 3. Back the warrant when its authority could be challenged, with the backing the field accepts

*Source: Toulmin 1958 (Verheij, pp. 8–9).*

Backing supports the warrant, and becomes relevant when the warrant's legitimacy is challenged; Toulmin stressed that the kinds of backing differ from one field of argument to another (Verheij, pp. 8–9).

- Where a warrant is contestable (a model assumption, a causal mechanism, an analogy from another setting), ask what backs it: a cited study, a theory, a validation.

### 4. Say how strongly the data support the claim

*Source: Toulmin 1958 (Verheij, pp. 2, 6).*

The qualifier expresses the degree of force the data give the claim by way of the warrant, as with *presumably* (Verheij, pp. 2, 6).

- Check that the strength the claim is stated with matches the force of its data and warrant (see `rules/claims.md` for hedges and boosters).

### 5. State the exceptions

*Source: Toulmin 1958 (Verheij, pp. 2, 9–10).*

Rebuttals are conditions of exception for the argument: circumstances in which the warrant's authority would be set aside (Verheij, pp. 2, 9–10).

- For each main claim, ask under what conditions it would not hold, and whether the paper says so (in a Discussion, often as limitations).

### 6. Treat each figure and table as data for a claim

*Source: Toulmin 1958, p. 97 (Verheij, p. 3). Application to figures and tables is this skill's.*

In Toulmin's scheme, data are the facts appealed to as the foundation for a claim (p. 97, as quoted by Verheij, p. 3). A figure or table in a research paper presents data, so it should be the data for some claim the text makes.

- For each figure and table, name the claim in the text that it supports. Flag a figure or table that supports no claim, and ask the author whether it is needed, or belongs in supplementary material.
- For each main claim, check that its data are shown somewhere: in a figure, a table or the text. Flag a main claim whose data appear nowhere, and ask the author for them.

## Where Toulmin gives no rule

Toulmin gives no analogue of logical validity for evaluating arguments, and does not distinguish kinds of rebuttal or their effect (Verheij, pp. 2, 10–11). He does not prescribe how many claims a paper may make, where in a paper each element must appear, or how many figures and tables a paper should have; rule 6 applies his notion of data to figures and tables, and is this skill's application. Because the book was not available, this file covers only what the reporting sources give. This file adds nothing where Toulmin is silent.

## Example

*An illustration of this file's procedure, not Toulmin's.* Draft sentence, in the Discussion of an ecology paper: "Because the 2022 survey recorded more bird species than the 2012 survey, the restoration programme has reversed biodiversity loss in the region."

- **Claim and data (rule 1):** claim: the programme has reversed biodiversity loss in the region; data: more species were recorded in 2022 than in 2012.
- **Warrant (rule 2):** unstated: a higher species count in one later survey shows a lasting regional increase, caused by the programme. Many readers would doubt this.
- **Backing (rule 3):** ask what supports the step: comparable survey effort and methods in both years, counts from more years, or trends at sites outside the programme.
- **Qualifier and rebuttal (rules 4–5):** "reversed" is unqualified, and no exception is stated (for example, a change in survey effort, or a regional trend that would have happened anyway). Ask the author.

## Validation checklist

- [ ] Each main claim was identified, with its data (rule 1)
- [ ] Each warrant was written out, and contestable ones were raised with the author (rule 2)
- [ ] Contestable warrants have backing of a kind the field accepts, or the author was asked for it (rule 3)
- [ ] Each claim's strength matches its data and warrant (rule 4)
- [ ] Exceptions to each main claim are stated, or the author was asked (rule 5)
- [ ] Each figure and table supports a named claim, and each main claim's data are shown somewhere (rule 6)
- [ ] No data, warrant, backing or exception was invented

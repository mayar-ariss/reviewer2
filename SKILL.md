# Swales: research article introductions

This skill applies John M. Swales's analysis of English research article introductions, the Create-a-Research-Space (CARS) model, to an introduction the user is writing or revising.

**Literature reviews:** for a review chapter, a proposal's review, or a review section, read `rules/literature-review.md`, drawn from Feak and Swales's *Telling a Research Story* (2009). For a review inside an introduction, use it together with rules 3–5 below.

**Every rule below is Swales's.** Each names its source by work and page. Where Swales gives no rule, this file says so rather than supplying one. Nothing here is a general writing preference.

## How to apply this skill

*Procedure, not a Swales rule.* Swales and Feak's method is rhetorical consciousness-raising: analyse how texts in the genre work before writing (Swales & Feak 2012, Introduction to the Third Edition). So the agent works in this order:

1. **Ask for the field and target journal**, or read them from the draft. Several rules below depend on field (rules 5, 11, 12).
2. **Label the draft.** Mark each sentence with its move and step (rule 1). Show the user the labelled draft.
3. **Check the moves against rules 1–12**, in order, and list what is missing, misplaced or unsignalled.
4. **Propose revisions as move-level changes**, citing the rule. Keep the author's claims, data and citations. Do not invent findings, gaps or references; where a move needs content the draft does not have (a citation for Move 1, a stated gap for Move 2), ask the author for it.
5. **Run the checklist** at the end.

## The model

Swales gives two versions. Use the 2004 version as the default; the 1990 steps are finer-grained options within it.

**Swales 2004 (revised CARS model, pp. 230, 232)**

| Move | Steps |
|---|---|
| **Move 1. Establishing a territory** (citations required) | Topic generalizations of increasing specificity |
| **Move 2. Establishing a niche** (citations possible) | Step 1A Indicating a gap, *or* Step 1B Adding to what is known; Step 2 (optional) Presenting positive justification |
| **Move 3. Presenting the present work** | Step 1 (obligatory) Announcing present research descriptively and/or purposively; Step 2 (optional) Presenting research questions or hypotheses; Step 3 (optional) Definitional clarifications; Step 4 (optional) Summarizing methods; Step 5 (probable in some fields) Announcing principal outcomes; Step 6 (probable in some fields) Stating the value of the present research; Step 7 (probable in some fields) Outlining the structure of the paper |

**Swales 1990 (original CARS model, p. 141)**

| Move | Steps |
|---|---|
| **Move 1. Establishing a territory** | Step 1 Claiming centrality and/or Step 2 Making topic generalization(s) and/or Step 3 Reviewing items of previous research |
| **Move 2. Establishing a niche** | Step 1A Counter-claiming, *or* 1B Indicating a gap, *or* 1C Question-raising, *or* 1D Continuing a tradition |
| **Move 3. Occupying the niche** | Step 1A Outlining purposes *or* 1B Announcing present research; Step 2 Announcing principal findings; Step 3 Indicating research article structure |

## Rules

### 1. Build the introduction from the three moves, in order

*Source: Swales 1990, pp. 141–142; Swales 2004, pp. 230, 232; Swales & Feak 1994, pp. 174–175 (Table 21).*

Swales models the introduction as three moves: establish a research territory, establish a niche in it, and occupy the niche with the present work (1990, p. 141). He draws the terms from ecology: researchers compete for space as organisms compete for a niche (1990, p. 142). Swales and Feak name two competitions the pattern answers, one for research space and one for readers (1994, pp. 174–175).

In AWG's version of the model, reviewing previous research (Move 1), establishing the niche (Move 2) and stating the purpose or nature of the present work (Move 3) are obligatory; showing that the area matters, announcing findings and outlining the paper are optional (1994, Table 21, p. 175).

- Each sentence belongs to a move; label it.
- An introduction missing an obligatory element is incomplete by this model.

### 2. Put the purpose statement near the end, not at the start

*Source: Swales & Feak 1994, pp. 173–175.*

Opening with a statement of purpose suits term papers and case reports, but Swales and Feak found it rare in published research papers, probably under one in ten; purpose statements usually come at or near the end of the introduction (p. 173). Their example of a paper that opens on its own study shows the cost: the specific opening appeals to the few readers already working on the topic and may lose the rest of the journal's readers (pp. 174–175).

- In a research paper, the first sentences belong to Move 1.
- The purposive or descriptive statement (rule 9) comes at or near the end of the introduction.

### 3. Move 1: show the territory, from general to specific, with citations

*Source: Swales 1990, pp. 141, 144; Swales 2004, p. 230; Swales & Feak 1994, pp. 175, 178–179.*

Move 1 can open by claiming centrality (1990, Step 1, p. 141; examples p. 144). In AWG this element shows that the research area is important, central, interesting, problematic or relevant in some way, and it is optional (1994, Table 21, p. 175). Swales and Feak note that these strong opening statements often use the present perfect (1994, pp. 178–179).

The 2004 revision folds the 1990 steps into topic generalizations of increasing specificity, and marks Move 1 as requiring citations (2004, p. 230).

- Move from the general area to the specific topic the paper addresses.
- Move 1 cites previous work; an uncited Move 1 does not meet the 2004 model.

### 4. Review the literature in an order the reader can follow

*Source: Swales & Feak 1994, pp. 179–183, including their summary of Swales 1990 on why writers cite (p. 181).*

Reviewing items of previous research is obligatory in AWG's model (1994, p. 179). Among the theories of why writers cite, Swales's own is that describing what has been done points to what has not, and so prepares a space for the new research (1994, p. 181). The key to a review, in Swales and Feak's account, is that order is imposed on the material for the reader, not only in the writer's mind (1994, p. 181). Chronological order can be a weak ordering; grouping the work into categories is one alternative (1994, pp. 181–183).

- Group the cited work so the grouping leads toward the niche in Move 2.
- Do not list studies one after another without an organizing principle.

### 5. Choose citation form and tense by what the sentence is about

*Source: Swales 1990, p. 148; Swales & Feak 1994, pp. 183–185; Swales & Feak 2012, pp. 344–346.*

Swales distinguishes **integral** citations, where the cited author is part of the sentence, from **non-integral** citations, where the author appears in parentheses or as a number (1990, p. 148). Most citations are non-integral (Swales & Feak 2012, p. 346).

Swales and Feak describe three tense patterns that cover about two-thirds of citing statements, offered as general guidelines rather than rules (1994, p. 183; 2012, p. 344):

| Pattern | Reference to | Tense |
|---|---|---|
| I | A single study, with the researcher as agent | Past |
| II | An area of inquiry, researcher not as agent | Present perfect |
| III | The state of current knowledge, no reference to researchers | Present |

Patterns I and II put the attention on what researchers did; Pattern III on what was found. Patterns I and II are most common in the humanities and least common in science, engineering and medicine; extensive reviews mix all three (1994, p. 183; 2012, p. 345).

With verbs for what a researcher did (investigated, studied, analysed), the past is in general obligatory. With verbs for what a researcher wrote or thought (stated, concluded, claimed), past, present perfect and present are all possible; moving from past toward present signals that the cited work is closer to the writer's own view, research or current knowledge. The present is also used for famous or important sources, the citational present (1994, pp. 184–185; 2012, p. 345).

### 6. Move 2: make the niche, briefly and clearly

*Source: Swales & Feak 1994, pp. 185–187; Swales 2004, pp. 230, 232; Swales 1990, pp. 141, 154.*

Swales and Feak call Move 2 the key move: the hinge between what has been done (Move 1) and what the present research is about (Move 3). It gives the motivation for the study, and by its end the reader should be able to anticipate Move 3. Most Move 2s indicate a gap, showing that the research story is not yet complete; they are usually short, often a single sentence, though some are longer critiques (1994, pp. 185–187).

The ways to establish the niche:

- **Swales 2004 (pp. 230, 232):** indicate a gap, or add to what is known; optionally present a positive justification for the study.
- **Swales 1990 (pp. 141, 154):** counter-claim, indicate a gap, raise a question, or continue a tradition.

### 7. Signal the start of Move 2

*Source: Swales 2004, p. 229; Swales 1990, pp. 154–155; Swales & Feak 1994, pp. 187, 189; Swales & Feak 2012, p. 350.*

Swales observes that a gap is often signalled by an adversative connector together with negative or quasi-negative language (2004, p. 229; 1990, pp. 154–155). The commonest way, in Swales and Feak's account, is a negative subject, which tells the reader at once that Move 1 has ended (1994, p. 189; 2012, p. 350):

- *little* with uncountable nouns (information, attention, work, research) and *few* with countable ones (studies, investigations, attempts). *A little* and *a few* are neutral, not negative.
- *no* when the conclusion rests on the literature without referring to it directly; *none of* when referring directly to the studies.

Negative verbs and adjectives range from neutral or slightly negative to strongly negative, and Swales and Feak advise care in choosing them (1994, p. 187).

Not every Move 2 needs an obvious gap (1994, p. 189):

- **A contrastive statement**, for writers who prefer to avoid negative or quasi-negative comment: research has focused on one thing rather than another.
- **Raising a question, a hypothesis or a need**, common in the "harder" fields; here the connector need not be of the *however* type.
- **Continuing a line of research**, which Swales and Feak find largely restricted to groups following up their own or similar groups' work.

### 8. In long introductions, Move 1 and Move 2 may cycle

*Source: Swales 1990, pp. 162–163.*

Swales notes that in longer introductions Move 1 and Move 2 can recur in cycles rather than occurring once each. A cycle still follows the pattern: territory, then niche.

### 9. Move 3: open with a purposive or descriptive statement that points to the present text

*Source: Swales & Feak 1994, p. 191; Swales 2004, p. 232; Swales 1990, p. 159.*

Move 3 turns the niche into the space the present paper occupies (1990, p. 159). Its first element is obligatory and has two variants: **purposive**, stating the main purpose or purposes, or **descriptive**, describing the main feature of the research (1994, p. 191; 2004, Step 1, p. 232).

Move 3 is usually signalled by a reference to the present text: *this*, *the present*, *reported*, *here*. These signals come early in the sentence. Where the field or journal allows, writers may switch from impersonal to personal *we* (1994, p. 191).

### 10. Tense in the purpose statement follows what you name

*Source: Swales & Feak 1994, p. 191; Swales & Feak 2012, p. 356.*

- **Naming the text** (paper, article, thesis, report): use the present. The past would suggest an original aim that has since changed.
- **Naming the investigation** (study, experiment, survey): past or present are both possible; the present is increasingly common and is the safe choice.

### 11. Add the optional Move 3 elements only where the field or paper needs them

*Source: Swales 2004, p. 232; Swales & Feak 1994, pp. 193–194.*

Swales 2004 lists research questions or hypotheses, definitional clarifications and a summary of methods as optional, and principal outcomes, the value of the research and the structure of the paper as probable in some fields only (p. 232). Swales and Feak note that the elements after the purpose statement are typically needed in longer texts such as theses, dissertations and long research papers, and may not be needed in short ones (1994, p. 193).

- **Secondary aims or features:** a second sentence can complete the purpose statement (1994, p. 193).
- **Value:** if you state the contribution here, be cautious and qualify the claim; it will be argued in the Discussion anyway (1994, p. 193).
- **Principal findings:** practice varies by field. Swales and Najjar (1987) found physicists include them about half the time and educational researchers hardly ever. If the paper has an abstract, Swales and Feak see no need to give the main findings a third time; otherwise follow the field's practice (1994, p. 193).
- **Structure of the paper:** obligatory in dissertations, but in research papers only in certain cases, such as a paper that does not follow the IMRD format or one in a new field (Cooper 1985 found it common in computer technology). Ask whether readers need it; vary the sentence structure of the outline (1994, p. 194).

### 12. Fit the model to the field and the journal

*Source: Swales 2004, p. 226; Swales & Feak 1994, p. 193; Swales 2004, pp. 230, 232.*

Swales describes the CARS model as fitting best where originality, especially in theory, is highly prized, competition is fierce, and academic promotion is strong (2004, p. 226). His 2004 revision of the model accommodates variation reported across fields (pp. 230, 232). Where practice varies, Swales and Feak's advice is to follow standard practice in the field (1994, p. 193).

- Read recent introductions in the target journal and compare their moves with the draft.
- Treat steps marked optional or probable in some fields as choices for that field, not requirements.

## Where Swales gives no rule

Swales gives no fixed length for an introduction or for each move, beyond noting that Move 2 is usually short (rule 6). He prescribes no number of citations, no paragraph count and no required opening sentence. His tense patterns are general guidelines, not rules (rule 5). The model describes research article introductions in English; for other part-genres (thesis introductions, proposals, grant applications) Swales and Feak treat introductions separately (Feak & Swales 2011), and this skill does not cover them. This skill adds nothing where Swales is silent.

## Validation checklist

- [ ] Every sentence is labelled with a move and step (rule 1)
- [ ] The introduction opens with Move 1, not with the purpose statement (rule 2)
- [ ] Move 1 moves from general to specific and cites previous work (rule 3)
- [ ] The literature is grouped in an order that leads to the niche (rule 4)
- [ ] Citation forms and tenses match what each sentence reports (rule 5)
- [ ] Move 2 is present, brief, and states a gap, an addition, a question or a continuation (rule 6)
- [ ] The shift into Move 2 is signalled (rule 7)
- [ ] Move 3 opens with a purposive or descriptive statement that refers to the present text (rule 9)
- [ ] The purpose statement's tense matches whether it names the text or the investigation (rule 10)
- [ ] Optional Move 3 elements are included only where the field or paper needs them (rule 11)
- [ ] The move pattern has been compared with recent introductions in the target journal (rule 12)
- [ ] No claim, gap, finding or citation was invented; content the draft lacks was requested from the author

# Methods: Swales and Feak on condensed and extended sections

Load this file when the user is writing or revising the Methods section of a research article, thesis, dissertation or proposal (also headed Materials and Methods, Methodology, Experimental Procedures or Study Design).

**Every rule below is Swales's, or Swales and Feak's.** Their books were not available to check, so rules cite them as reported in named open sources, and cite Swales's own talk abstract directly. Where they give no rule, this file says so rather than supplying one. Nothing here is a general writing preference.

## How to apply this file

*Procedure, not a rule of Swales and Feak.* Their central distinction is between condensed and extended Methods sections (rule 1), so the agent works in this order:

1. **Ask for the field, the target journal and its readership**, or read them from the draft.
2. **Read recent Methods sections in the target journal** and place them on the condensed–extended range (rule 1).
3. **Place the draft on the same range,** feature by feature (rules 2–7), and list where it departs from the journal's practice.
4. **Propose revisions citing the rule.** Keep the author's procedures, materials and citations. Do not invent steps, settings, justifications or sources; where the section needs them, ask the author.
5. **Run the checklist** at the end.

## The range

Swales and Feak contrast condensed and extended Methods sections (AWG, Table 1, as reproduced by Enago):

| Condensed | Extended |
|---|---|
| Assumes background knowledge | Sees need to provide background |
| Avoids named subsections | Several named subsections |
| Uses acronyms and citations as shorthand | Uses descriptions |
| Running series of verbs | Usually one finite verb per clause |
| Few *by* + *-ing* "how" statements | A number of "how" statements |
| Few definitions and examples | More definitions and examples |
| Few justifications | Several justifications (often initial purpose clauses) |
| Few linking phrases | Wide range of linking phrases |

## Rules

### 1. Place the section on the condensed–extended range the field expects

*Source: AWG (Bruce; SUU, citing p. 224; Enago); Swales 2008.*

In Bruce's account of AWG, Swales and Feak describe Methods sections in the social sciences as tending to be slow, or extended, and those in the physical sciences, such as medicine, as tending to be fast, or compressed; the metaphors refer to how much detail the section gives in describing and justifying the design and process (Bruce). Methods sections vary in how much information and explanation they contain (AWG, p. 224, as cited by SUU). The condensed approach suits fields where standard methods are widely available, such as the natural sciences, engineering and parts of medical research; the extended approach suits many social sciences (Enago). Swales argues that it is in Methods sections that research articles vary most across disciplines (Swales 2008).

- Decide first how condensed or extended the target journal's Methods sections are, and judge the draft against that, not against a single standard.

### 2. Assume only the background your readers have

*Source: AWG, Table 1 (Enago); Swales 1990, pp. 167, 169 (Marcuzzo); Swales 2004, p. 220 (Marcuzzo).*

A condensed section assumes background knowledge; an extended one sees a need to provide it (AWG, Table 1). Swales reports that in biochemistry, biology and medicine, methods are often named rather than described (1990, p. 167), so the Methods section in these fields tends to be enigmatic, relies on the reader's prior knowledge and is not designed for easy replication (1990, p. 169, as reported by Marcuzzo). He describes Methods sections as synthetic texts that presuppose the reader's knowledge of the methodology (2004, p. 220, as reported by Marcuzzo).

- Ask who will read the section. Where readers may come from outside the specialism, check that each named procedure is described or can be found.

### 3. Choose between shorthand and description

*Source: AWG, Table 1 (Enago).*

Condensed sections use acronyms and citations as shorthand for procedures; extended sections use descriptions. Condensed sections give few definitions and examples; extended sections give more (AWG, Table 1).

- For each acronym and each "as described in [citation]", decide whether the target readership can follow it, and ask the author to describe or define where it cannot.

### 4. Use named subsections where the section is extended

*Source: AWG, Table 1 (Enago).*

Condensed sections avoid named subsections; extended sections have several (AWG, Table 1).

- Check the draft's subsections against the journal's practice.

### 5. Say how things were done, in clauses the reader can follow

*Source: AWG, Table 1 (Enago).*

Condensed sections run a series of verbs together and make few *by* + *-ing* statements of how something was done; extended sections usually have one finite verb per clause and a number of "how" statements (AWG, Table 1).

- In an extended section, break running series of verbs into clauses, and check that each key step says how it was carried out.

### 6. Justify choices in proportion to how extended the section is

*Source: AWG, Table 1 (Enago); Bruce.*

Condensed sections give few justifications; extended sections give several, often as initial purpose clauses (AWG, Table 1). Justifying the design and process is part of what makes a section extended (Bruce).

- In an extended section, check that each non-obvious choice says why it was made, for example with an opening purpose clause (*To test whether…*, *In order to…*).
- Ask the author for the reason; do not supply one.

### 7. Link the steps

*Source: AWG, Table 1 (Enago).*

Condensed sections use few linking phrases; extended sections use a wide range (AWG, Table 1).

- In an extended section, check that the order and relation of steps is marked.

### 8. Remember that the section reconstructs what was done

*Source: Swales 2004, pp. 96–97 (Marcuzzo).*

Swales observes that a Methods section is often a reconstruction that differs from what actually happened, leaving out errors, missed opportunities and abandoned analyses; a more faithful account, including such setbacks, would help less experienced researchers (2004, pp. 96–97, as reported by Marcuzzo).

- Where a departure from plan bears on the results (an exclusion, a changed analysis, a failed condition), ask the author whether the section should report it.

## Where Swales and Feak give no rule

In Bruce's account, Swales and Feak offer no generic move structure for Methods sections (Bruce), and this file supplies none. The sources used here give no Swales rule on tense or voice in Methods sections, no required subsections and no length. Swales's checklist of features that may occur in Methods sections (Swales 2008) is described in his talk abstract but was not available. This file adds nothing where Swales and Feak are silent.

## Example

*An illustration of this file's procedure, not Swales and Feak's.* Draft sentence, in a Methods section written for an interdisciplinary journal: "Samples were digested, filtered and analysed by ICP-MS as in [12]."

- **Range (rule 1):** the sentence is condensed: shorthand, a running series of verbs, no "how" statements and no justification. Check whether the journal's Methods sections are this condensed.
- **Background (rules 2–3):** *ICP-MS* and *as in [12]* assume the reader knows the technique and the cited protocol. If the readership is interdisciplinary, ask the author to spell out the acronym and give the key conditions.
- **How and why (rules 5–6):** if the journal's sections are extended, ask how each step was done and why this method was chosen.

## Validation checklist

- [ ] The target journal's Methods sections were placed on the condensed–extended range, and the draft judged against them (rule 1)
- [ ] The background assumed matches the readership (rule 2)
- [ ] Acronyms and citation shorthand suit the readership, or are described (rule 3)
- [ ] Subsections follow the journal's practice (rule 4)
- [ ] In an extended section, steps are in clear clauses and say how they were done (rule 5)
- [ ] In an extended section, non-obvious choices are justified (rule 6)
- [ ] In an extended section, steps are linked (rule 7)
- [ ] Departures from plan that bear on the results were raised with the author (rule 8)
- [ ] No step, setting, justification or source was invented

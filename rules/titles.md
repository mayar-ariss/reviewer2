# Titles: Hyland and Zou on research article titles

Load this file when the user is writing or revising the title of a research article, thesis or dissertation.

**Every rule below is Hyland and Zou's.** Their article is open access and was read in full; rules cite its page numbers. Where they give no rule, this file says so rather than supplying one. Nothing here is a general writing preference.

*Sources and how they are cited are listed at the end of this file.*

## How to apply this file

*Procedure, not a rule of Hyland and Zou's.*

1. **Ask for the field and the target journal**, and read the titles of recent articles in it (rule 5).
2. **Describe the draft title:** its length in words, whether it is a statement or a question, whether it is single or compound, and whether it gives only the topic or also the approach or a result (rules 2–4).
3. **Compare it with the field's patterns** and with the journal's titles, and list where it departs from them.
4. **Propose revisions citing the rule.** Do not add a method, site or result the author has not stated; ask which, if any, the title should carry.
5. **Run the checklist** at the end.

## The categories

Hyland and Zou describe titles along three dimensions (H&Z 2022, pp. 5–9):

| Dimension | Options |
|---|---|
| **Form** | Indicative (a statement or phrase) or interrogative (a question) |
| **Structure** | Single, or compound: two parts separated by a non-alphanumeric character, usually a colon |
| **Content** | Descriptive: indicates only the subject of the article; or detailed: also states the main findings or the approach |

## Rules

### 1. Make the title both informative and appealing

*Source: H&Z 2022, pp. 1–3.*

Titles name the work and attract readers, who increasingly find papers through keyword searches rather than journal contents pages; a title therefore affects whether a paper is read, cited and used (pp. 1–2). Hyland and Zou describe the writer's task as making titles both informative and appealing (p. 1). Writers are commonly advised to make titles clear, informative and specific, and to fill them with searchable keywords (p. 2).

- Check that the title names what the paper is about in the terms a reader in the field would search for.

### 2. Use a question only where the field uses questions

*Source: H&Z 2022, p. 6.*

Questions make up 8.5% of titles in the corpus: about 24% in education and 14% in applied linguistics, against 2.8% in mechanical engineering, 1.1% in biochemistry and 0.2% in mathematics (p. 6).

- In the sciences and engineering, flag a question title and ask whether the target journal uses them.

### 3. Choose between a single and a compound title by the field's practice

*Source: H&Z 2022, pp. 5–7.*

Single titles make up 55% of the corpus (p. 6). Compounds make up 82% of indicative titles in history but 5% in mathematics (p. 7).

- Check whether the target journal's titles are mostly single or compound, and whether the draft follows that pattern.

### 4. Decide what detail the title gives beyond the topic

*Source: H&Z 2022, pp. 8–9.*

Most compound titles are descriptive, giving only the subject (p. 8). Where titles do give detail, it is mostly the approach or method; results are relatively rare, presumably because authors do not want to give too much away (p. 9). The share of detailed indicative titles varies by field, from 42.5% in education and 32.8% in mechanical engineering to 11.1% in mathematics and 3.3% in history (pp. 8–9).

- Say whether the draft is descriptive or detailed. Where the field often names the method, ask whether the title should; where the title states a result, check it against the paper (see `rules/claims.md`).

### 5. Fit the length and explicitness to the field and its audience

*Source: H&Z 2022, pp. 5, 10.*

Average title length ranges from 14.2 words in education and 13.4 in applied linguistics to 9.7 in mechanical engineering and 9.0 in mathematics (p. 5). Hyland and Zou suggest that writers in the sciences work in relatively discrete, clearly identifiable areas, while social scientists must create rather than assume an audience, which calls for longer, more explicit titles (p. 10). They conclude that one size does not fit all, and that the discipline and the target journal should guide the choice (p. 10).

- Compare the draft's length and explicitness with the target journal's titles, not with a single standard.

## Where Hyland and Zou give no rule

The study examines formal features only, not qualities such as cleverness or density, and does not test whether title features affect citations. Hyland and Zou find style-guide advice on titles often vague or contradictory (p. 10), and give no rule on wording beyond the patterns above. Their engineering data come from mechanical engineering; other engineering fields may differ. This file adds nothing where they are silent.

## Example

*An illustration of this file's procedure, not Hyland and Zou's.* Draft title, for a mechanical engineering journal: "The Importance of Lubrication in Gear Failure".

- **Description (rules 2–4):** six words; indicative, single, descriptive.
- **Field (rules 4–5):** in mechanical engineering, a third of indicative titles give detail, usually the approach. Ask whether the title should name the method or the case (for example, a finite-element model of a particular gearbox), and check the target journal's titles.
- **Informativeness (rule 1):** *the importance of* names no searchable content. Ask which terms a reader would search for (see also `rules/informativeness.md`).

## Validation checklist

- [ ] The title names the paper's subject in searchable terms (rule 1)
- [ ] A question title fits the field and the journal (rule 2)
- [ ] Single or compound structure follows the journal's practice (rule 3)
- [ ] Any detail (approach or result) is the author's and is supported by the paper (rule 4)
- [ ] Length and explicitness fit the field and the journal (rule 5)
- [ ] No method, site or result was added without the author

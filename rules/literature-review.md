# Literature reviews: Feak and Swales

Load this file when the user is writing or revising a literature review: a review chapter in a thesis or dissertation, the literature review of a proposal, or the review of previous work inside a research article.

## Source and how it is cited

| Short form | Work |
|---|---|
| F&S | Feak, C. B., & Swales, J. M. (2009). *Telling a Research Story: Writing a Literature Review*. Ann Arbor: University of Michigan Press. |

Every rule is drawn from this book and cited with page numbers. Where Feak and Swales report another researcher's finding (Hyland, Charles, Boote and Beile), the rule says so; the finding is theirs, the use of it is Feak and Swales's. Where Feak and Swales give no rule, this file says so rather than supplying one.

The book covers student genres (review chapters, dissertation proposals, dissertations) most fully, and the review sections of research articles as well (F&S, p. xiv). For a review inside a research article introduction, apply this file together with rules 3–5 in `rules/introduction.md`.

## How to apply this file

*Procedure, not a Swales rule.* Feak and Swales work top-down: first the purpose and organization of the review, then citation, stance and wording (F&S, p. 76). The agent follows the same order:

1. Ask what the review is for: the research questions or hypotheses it leads to, and the genre (chapter, proposal, article section) and field.
2. Check purpose and organization (rules 1–5), then the opening and the links between sections (rules 6–8), then citation and tense (rules 9–12), then stance and wording (rules 13–15).
3. Keep the author's sources and claims. Do not add references, findings or evaluations the author has not made; ask for them.
4. Finish with the questions in rule 16.

## Rules

### 1. Know why the review is there

*Source: F&S, pp. 2, 6, 8.*

Feak and Swales give three reasons for reviewing the literature: to avoid repeating work others have already done; to show how the present work is situated within, builds on, or departs from earlier work; and to show that the writer belongs to the field (p. 2). A review therefore organizes previous work in relation to the writer's own planned or actual research, and its priorities depend on that research (p. 6). They also present Swales's own account of citation: describing what has been done points to what has not, and so prepares a space for new research (p. 8).

### 2. Make the review lead to the need for your research

*Source: F&S, pp. 10, 19, 21.*

A frequent criticism of reviews in article manuscripts is that the review does not contribute to the argumentative shape of the introduction: it does not lead to the conclusion that the new research is relevant (p. 10). One goal of a review is to make a point or answer a question, guiding the reader to see the need for the research the writer has done or intends to do (pp. 19, 21). A review is more than a listing of previous work; the choice of what to include and exclude establishes the context for the writer's contribution (p. 21).

### 3. Avoid the weaknesses advisors report

*Source: F&S, pp. 10–11.*

Feak and Swales compile the comments advisors most often make on draft reviews (pp. 10–11). A draft is weak when it:

- lists previous papers without showing which are more and less significant, or where the writer stands;
- gives a chronological account that does not preface the writer's own research, with the research question not shaping how the literature is characterized;
- treats theory and recent empirical work in two parts with no coherent relation between them;
- describes each study piece by piece, like an anthology, without evaluating and connecting them;
- in an interdisciplinary review, keeps each body of work within its original discipline instead of making connections across them.

Reviews may also be criticized for missing inputs or for far too many references (p. 10).

### 4. Impose an order on the literature

*Source: F&S, pp. 12–18.*

Order is imposed by categorizing the literature by aspects of the work (pp. 12–13). Feak and Swales's case study sorts 27 papers by date, country of origin, field, the author's perspective on the concept, and type of publication, then regroups them by the category that proves most illuminating for the purpose, in that case perspective (pp. 13–15). Other fields will need other categories, such as theoretical framework, type of study or sample size (p. 17).

As a preparatory step they recommend a matrix, chart, tree diagram or table, or a mind map (p. 16). Such devices let the writer survey the literature at a glance, encourage connections, help avoid low-level comparisons, and point to the common threads that give a bird's-eye view of complex material (p. 17).

Thematic organization does not mean abandoning chronology: within each category, time order can work well, especially when tracing the development of a concept or a technology (p. 18). The first grouping will rarely be the final one; papers chosen early may later be dropped (p. 18).

### 5. Say how the literature was selected, where the field expects it

*Source: F&S, pp. 20, 26, 32.*

Readers may expect particular work to appear in a review, and questions about what was left out can reach the dissertation defense (p. 26). So, depending on the field, the writer may need to explain how the papers were identified for inclusion (p. 26). An advisor's comment in Feak and Swales's case study asks for the selection criteria before the findings (p. 20). For doctoral candidates, who are not assumed to know the literature, Feak and Swales believe that clarifying how the literature was selected is a way to demonstrate command of it (p. 32). Systematic reviews, common in the health sciences, make their inclusion and exclusion criteria fully transparent (p. 3).

### 6. Give the review an introduction, a body and a conclusion

*Source: F&S, pp. 23, 41.*

A typical review has an introduction, a body and a conclusion. The introduction may open with a general description of the topic and show that it is interesting, problematic or otherwise relevant; it may then establish that a review is valuable for understanding the research area; and at its end it clarifies the scope and overall organization of the review (p. 23). One way to begin the first paragraph is a generalization, accepted knowledge in the field, or widely known information (p. 23). At the end, the writer may recapitulate what the review has accomplished (p. 41).

### 7. Use metadiscourse to guide the reader, more in longer and more complex reviews

*Source: F&S, pp. 38–40.*

Metadiscourse is text about the text itself: it adds no content, but reveals organization, highlights important parts and evaluates, and its main role is to reduce the reader's cognitive load (p. 38). Feak and Swales list factors that affect how much is used (pp. 38–39):

- more in longer texts, which are not read in one sitting, so more in books, dissertations and theses;
- more in expository than narrative texts;
- some variation across disciplines;
- more for complex than straightforward material;
- more at the beginnings and ends of sections and chapters;
- attitudes vary across cultures, and some academic cultures consider extensive metadiscourse offensive to the reader.

In a review it typically takes three forms: projections of what is coming, orientations to the present point, and recapitulations of what has been covered (pp. 39–40).

### 8. Link the sections so the information flows

*Source: F&S, pp. 33–35.*

A challenge in a review is linking its sections so that the flow of information is smooth (p. 33). Feak and Swales's example closes one section by announcing the next and ends the following section the same way (pp. 33–35).

### 9. Decide, for each source, how to bring it in

*Source: F&S, pp. 43–45.*

When referring to prior work, the writer decides whether to quote, paraphrase or summarize, and which studies to discuss as a group and which alone. The citation patterns themselves are limited by the field's style guide and further by disciplinary preference (p. 43). Feak and Swales report Hyland's (1999) study of eight disciplines: summary and paraphrase dominated everywhere, and no quotations at all appeared in the science and engineering papers studied (pp. 44–45).

### 10. Use integral and non-integral citations for what each does

*Source: F&S, pp. 45–47.*

In an **integral** citation the cited author's name is a grammatical part of the sentence; in a **non-integral** citation it stands outside, in parentheses or as a number. Integral citations put the focus on the researcher (author prominent); non-integral ones on the research (research prominent) (p. 45).

- An integral citation with the authors as grammatical subject typically covers one or two studies, rarely three; a passive *by* phrase may take up to about three (p. 46).
- Feak and Swales report that in Hyland's study non-integral citations were the majority in every field but philosophy, while Charles (2006) found a marked preference for integral citations in dissertations (p. 46).
- Because a review is expected to discuss some previous work in detail, integral citations may predominate there; focusing on individual studies can help the writer show a perspective on the literature and position the research (p. 47).

### 11. Choose citation tense by what the sentence reports

*Source: F&S, pp. 47, 51–54.*

Feak and Swales offer only general guidelines, to be tested against writing in the writer's own field (p. 47). About two-thirds of citing statements fall into three patterns (pp. 51–52):

| Pattern | Reference to | Tense |
|---|---|---|
| I | A single study, often integral | Past |
| II | An area of inquiry, generally non-integral | Present perfect |
| III | Generally accepted knowledge in the field | Present |

Extensive reviews use all three, which adds variety (p. 52). Citing Charles (2006), they note that verbs of arguing, claiming and suggesting tend to take the present, and verbs of finding and showing the past (pp. 52–53). With verbs for what a researcher did, the past is by and large obligatory; with verbs for what a researcher wrote or thought, tense options exist, and moving from past toward present signals that the reported work is closer to the writer's own opinion, research or current knowledge (p. 53). The present is also used for famous or important sources (p. 54). Other patterns exist, such as *according to*, *as indicated by* and *in the view of* (p. 54).

### 12. Vary reporting verbs, and make each citation's meaning unambiguous

*Source: F&S, pp. 54–58.*

Good writers of reviews vary both their citation patterns and their reporting verbs (p. 54). Feak and Swales report Hyland's (1999) finding that, of the many reporting verbs in use, a small number predominate (p. 54). Their table of the most frequent verbs by discipline, based on Swales and Feak (2004), shows that the leading verbs differ by field, and so does the number of reporting verbs per paper: on average about seven in physics against 57 in philosophy, with more in the social sciences than in engineering and the hard sciences (pp. 54–55).

A citation can be ambiguous about whether the cited author claimed something or actually found or did it; one editor in their field refused to accept such "hanging" citations (p. 56). The risk is greatest in number systems with references placed at the ends of sentences (p. 56). Where a reader cannot tell which claim goes with which source, or whether a source is research or commentary, rewrite so the intended meaning is clear (pp. 56–58).

### 13. Take a stance toward the literature, and balance description with evaluation

*Source: F&S, pp. 66–73, 95.*

In many fields readers expect to see the writer's own perspective on the research in the field: what the writer thinks about it, not only what the writer knows (p. 70). Stance shows in evaluative words and in caution: characterizing results as inconsistent or as having broad consensus, describing work as groundbreaking, hedging claims, and engaging the reader (pp. 70–72). Feak and Swales present Hyland's (2005) account of the elements that reveal stance: hedges, boosters, attitude markers and personal pronouns (p. 73).

Their case study shows the limits. The advisor sends back a draft that is enthusiastic in tone, groups the studies by country without discussing any of them, and ends with a verdict beyond the writer's purpose (p. 66); the advisor is pleased with the revision, which discusses the studies, qualifies its generalizations and leads into the next section (pp. 67–69). Whether description and evaluation are balanced is one of their closing questions (p. 95).

### 14. Tell the research story in your own words

*Source: F&S, pp. 76–82.*

Paraphrase restates specific points from a source; summary focuses on the main message (p. 76). Finding one's own words can be hard: the text may be highly technical, alternatives may not exist, the source may seem too well written to change, the writer may not fully understand it, or vocabulary may be limited (pp. 76–78). Feak and Swales still urge working toward one's own words, because that shows understanding and keeps a consistent style, which a string of quotations or reused phrases from several sources cannot (pp. 78–79).

- Borrowing language common in academic English, or common knowledge, is not plagiarism; standard phraseology and skeletal phrases of the field may be borrowed, but not special expressions or long strings specific to one publication (pp. 79–80).
- Famous phrases need quotation marks (p. 79).
- Where the line falls between plagiarism and acceptable writing is disputed; check the university's policy and the advisor's view (p. 82).

### 15. Paraphrase and summarize by working from the points, not the sentences

*Source: F&S, pp. 82–92.*

To paraphrase, first identify the relevant points and the relationships between them, then find linking expressions, possible synonyms and changes in part of speech, and decide what to add or delete (pp. 82–83). Choose synonyms with care: not every synonym fits every context, and collocations matter; Feak and Swales suggest checking an expression in Google Scholar, in quotation marks and with a wildcard if useful (pp. 85–86).

There are no guidelines for when to summarize, but a previous study that warrants detailed discussion will usually be summarized for the aspects that bear on the writer's own work (p. 86). A summary written sentence by sentence from the source stays too close to it; a better strategy is to identify the points needed to support the writer's claim, for example by asking what was done, how, by whom, and what is new or advantageous, and answering in one's own words, keeping technical vocabulary that cannot change (pp. 89–90).

- Depending on the field, identify the source author in the summary (p. 91).
- Use family names only in in-text references; in a longer summary, remind the reader that it is a summary and mention the author at points along the way (p. 92).

### 16. Close by checking the review against the research it serves

*Source: F&S, pp. 92–95.*

Feak and Swales offer Boote and Beile's (2005) criteria for evaluating a review, while noting that advisors will have their own expectations (pp. 92–93). They end with these questions for the writer (p. 95), which this skill uses as its checklist:

- [ ] The review is shaped to fit the research questions or hypotheses
- [ ] The sources are appropriately grouped
- [ ] Description and evaluation are balanced
- [ ] A long or complex review has enough metadiscourse to show the research story unfolding
- [ ] The review explains why certain sources were included or excluded
- [ ] Where appropriate for the field, both integral and non-integral citations are used
- [ ] Reporting verbs and structures are varied
- [ ] Citation tenses are chosen correctly
- [ ] No "missing inputs" a reader or reviewer is likely to notice; the research territory is covered
- [ ] The literature is described in an original manner, so questions of plagiarism do not arise

## Where Feak and Swales give no rule

They give no fixed length for a review, no number of sources, and no single required organization; the categories for grouping depend on the field and purpose (rule 4). Whether a thesis review is a separate chapter or spread through the work, and whether an article's review is part of the introduction or a section of its own, they leave as questions for the field and the advisor (p. 4). Their tense patterns are guidelines, not rules (rule 11). This file adds nothing where they are silent.

## Example

*An illustration of this file's procedure, not Feak and Swales's.* Draft review paragraph: "Smith (2015) used a one-dimensional model. Jones (2017) used a two-dimensional model. Lee (2019) studied channel roughness. Many studies have modelled floods."

- **Purpose (rules 1–2):** the paragraph lists studies but does not lead to a need for the present research. Ask the author which research question it serves.
- **Order (rule 4):** the studies are ordered by date. Ask which grouping fits the research question, for example by model type or by what was calibrated.
- **Citations (rule 10):** every citation is integral, which puts the researchers rather than the findings in focus. Check whether that is what the author intends.
- **Stance (rule 13):** nothing evaluates the studies. Ask what the author makes of each, and how they bear on the gap.
- For "many studies", see `rules/vagueness.md`.

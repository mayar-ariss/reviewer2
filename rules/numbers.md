# Numbers: Miller on writing about numbers

Load this file when the user wants the numbers in a draft checked. For each number that matters, it asks whether the number has its context and units, whether the text says what it means, what it is compared with and judged against, whether its digits suit the measurement, and whether it is plausible. Where the draft claims statistical significance, it checks that the words match the test result. It applies to any part of a research article, thesis or dissertation that states numbers.

**Every rule below rests on Miller.** Her books were not available to check in full. The rules are drawn from her study guides, which set exercises for each chapter of the books and state what she asks a writer or a reviewer to do, and from a published review; they are cited by page and item. Rule 9 rests on what her exercises work with rather than on what they ask, and says so. Where she gives no rule, this file says so rather than supplying one. Nothing here is a general writing preference.

*Sources and how they are cited are listed at the end of this file.*

## How to apply this file

*Procedure, not a rule of Miller.*

1. **Find the numbers the claims rest on:** the values, differences, ratios, percentages and test results in the passage's claims, and in a Results section the numbers its commentary highlights. In a long draft, start with these (see `SKILL.md`, *How to apply any rules file*, step 2) and check other numbers only where a check finds a problem.
2. **For each, check its context and units** (rules 1–2), then **what it means and what it is compared with or judged against** (rules 3–5).
3. **Check its digits and whether it is plausible**, and that it agrees wherever it appears (rules 6–7).
4. **Where the text speaks of significance**, apply rules 8–9.
5. **Propose revisions citing the rule.** Never supply a value, unit, reference value, standard or test result; where one is missing, ask the author for it.
6. **Run the checklist** at the end.

## Rules

### 1. Give each number its context

*Source: Miller 2015, ch. 2, as reported in SG 2015, pp. 5–6.*

Miller's study guide asks a reviewer whether the context of a study is specified and, if not, which of the Ws (such as who, what, when and where) are missing or poorly defined (Suggested course extensions 2, A.1, p. 5); a description of an association should give the Ws, the units, the direction and the magnitude (B.1, p. 6).

- For each number a claim rests on, check that the reader can tell what was measured, for which cases, when and where. Flag a number whose case, period or place cannot be recovered from its sentence or paragraph, and ask the author for it.

### 2. State the units, and make them consistent before comparing

*Source: Miller 2015, chs. 2 and 4, as reported in SG 2015, pp. 6, 15 and 21, and in Bergman.*

A description of numbers should give their units (Suggested course extensions 2, B.1, p. 6), and Bergman quotes Miller's warning that even rocket scientists make basic, easily avoidable mistakes about units. Her exercises ask the writer to state the system of measurement, the unit of analysis and the scale (for example, dollars or thousands of dollars) (Suggested course extensions 4, A.1.c, p. 21), and to compare four specimens whose masses are given in pounds, grams, kilograms and ounces, asking what steps are needed before the comparison can be made (Problem set 4, problem 5, p. 15).

- Check that every value has its unit, including differences (in what unit). For the base of a percentage, see rule 4.
- Flag values compared across different units or scales, and the same quantity given in different units in different places.

### 3. Report each number and say what it means

*Source: Miller 2015, ch. 2, as reported in SG 2015, p. 5.*

The study guide asks whether the values are reported (in the text, a table or a chart) and whether they are interpreted in the text (Suggested course extensions 2, A.4.b–c), and whether the direction and magnitude of an association are specified (A.5).

- For each number a claim rests on, check that the text says what it shows: which way it goes and how large it is. Flag a number left for the reader to interpret.
- This rule concerns single numbers. What the commentary on a table or figure as a whole should say is in `rules/data-commentary.md` (rules 1 and 3).

### 4. Name the comparison and its reference value

*Source: Miller 2015, ch. 5, as reported in SG 2015, p. 31.*

Miller distinguishes types of quantitative comparison, among them rank, difference, ratio and percentage difference or change (Suggested course extensions 5, A.1.a). For each comparison the writer should identify the reference value, and whether it comes from the data or from another source, such as a historical value or a reference population (A.1.b); choose a comparison value suited to the topic and units (B.1.a); justify the type of comparison by the variable's level of measurement (B.1.c); and describe the results, not the mathematical steps of the comparison (A.1.d).

- For each comparison, name its type and its reference value: *5% of what*, *higher than what*. Flag a percentage or ratio whose base the reader cannot identify.
- Check that the type of comparison suits the variable's level of measurement, and ask the author where it may not.
- Flag text that walks through the arithmetic instead of stating the result.

### 5. Judge a value against a stated reference

*Source: Miller 2015, chs. 3 and 4, as reported in SG 2015, pp. 12, 16 and 21–22.*

Miller asks the writer to identify the standards or cutoffs needed to judge a value (Problem set 4, problem 7, p. 16) and, where a standard cutoff or pattern is used in the literature, to find a reference that explains how it is applied and interpreted (Suggested course extensions 4, A.3 and B.4, pp. 21–22). Whether an effect is big enough to matter is judged from background facts about the topic (Suggested course extensions 3, B.1.d, p. 12; see rule 8).

- Where the text calls a value good, poor, large, small, acceptable or accurate (*within 5%*, *close agreement*, *excellent*), check that it says what the value is judged against (a standard, a cutoff, or a reference value from another source such as earlier work) and gives the source.
- Ask the author for the reference; do not supply one.
- Where the judgement links a result to a broader claim (*agreement within 5% shows the model is reliable*), the link is a warrant: also apply `rules/argument.md` (rules 2–3).

### 6. Report digits and decimal places to suit the measurement

*Source: Miller 2015, ch. 4, as reported in SG 2015, pp. 15 and 21, and in Bergman.*

One of Miller's exercises gives a stopwatch accurate to the nearest tenth of a second and a nine-trial average of 10.44444444 seconds, and asks for a sentence reporting that average (Problem set 4, problem 3, p. 15); another asks for the appropriate number of digits and decimal places for reporting a mean in the text and in a table (Suggested course extensions 4, A.1.c, p. 21). The book gives guidelines on the number of digits and decimal places by type of statistic and table (Bergman).

- Flag values reported with more digits than the measurement or the method can support, and ask the author what precision the instrument, data or model supports.
- *This skill's application:* a value may be rounded differently in the text and in a table. Flag it only where the two disagree beyond rounding (rule 7).
- The number of digits for each type of statistic is in Miller's table, which was not available; see *Where Miller gives no rule*.

### 7. Check that each value is plausible and agrees with the data

*Source: Miller 2015, ch. 4, as reported in SG 2015, pp. 16, 18 and 22.*

Miller asks the writer to judge whether values make sense for their concepts and units (Problem set 4, problem 15, p. 18), and to consider the definitional limits on a variable and how the concept, the units and the study's context affect its plausible range (Suggested course extensions 4, B.3.c–f, p. 22). Another exercise asks whether sentences correctly reflect the table they describe (Problem set 4, problem 8, p. 16).

- Flag values outside their definitional limits (a percentage above 100 where it cannot be, a negative quantity that cannot be negative) or implausible for the concept, units and setting, and ask the author to confirm them.
- Check that each value in the text agrees with the table or figure it comes from. *This skill's application:* where a value disagrees, or repeats one from another row or column, ask the author to confirm it rather than correcting it (see `SKILL.md`, *How to apply any rules file*, step 4).

### 8. Keep statistical and substantive significance apart

*Source: Miller 2015, ch. 3, as reported in SG 2015, pp. 8, 12 and 13; Miller 2013, ch. 3, as reported in SG 2013, p. 11.*

Miller's chapter separates statistical significance from substantive significance. Her exercises ask whether statements correctly convey statistical significance and, if not, to rewrite them so the words match the numbers (Problem set 3, problem 1, p. 8; the same exercise opens SG 2013's chapter 3, p. 11). The solutions mark as incorrect a p-value of 0.95 read as near-certainty that a difference is not due to chance, a p-value read as the size of a change, and the claim that a larger sample would make the difference itself larger: test statistics and p-values indicate statistical significance and do not measure the size of an association, and sample size does not affect the size of a difference (Solutions 3, problem 1, p. 13). Substantive significance asks whether an effect is big enough to matter, judged from background facts about the topic (Problem set 3, problem 2, p. 8; Suggested course extensions 3, B.1.d–e, p. 12).

- Check that every statement about statistical significance matches the reported test result, and that a p-value or test statistic is not read as the size of an effect.
- Where the text calls an effect important or negligible, check that it gives the facts that show why (rule 5): a significant result is not by that alone an important one. Whose judgement it is, and how it is signalled, is checked in `rules/claims.md` (rule 7).
- In health research, also apply `rules/spin.md`.

### 9. Point to the test result behind a statement of significance

*Source: Miller 2013, ch. 11, as reported in SG 2013, pp. 86–89.*

*This rule rests on what Miller's exercises work with, not on a principle they state.* Her chapter "Choosing How to Present Statistical Results" sets exercises on the forms a test result can take: test statistics, p-values, confidence intervals, standard errors, and symbols that denote significance levels (for example, * for p < 0.05) (Problem set 11, problems 1, 7 and 10).

- Check that each statement that a difference or estimate is (or is not) significant is accompanied by, or points to, the result it rests on.
- Check that any symbols for significance levels are defined.
- Which form to report is the author's choice for the audience and the journal; Miller's chapter is about that choice. Do not prescribe one.

## Where Miller gives no rule

Miller's guidelines on digits and decimal places by type of statistic are in a table of the book (Bergman), which was not available; this file gives no digit counts. Because guidelines vary by discipline, Miller tells readers to consult an appropriate manual of style (as reported by Bergman): follow the target journal's style or style manual for digits, for numerals versus number words, and for number formats.

The sources used here do not cover the uncertainty of physical measurements (instrument and model error, uncertainty budgets). This file gives no rule for it. Fields that report measured quantities often follow the international *Guide to the Expression of Uncertainty in Measurement* (JCGM 100:2008); check what the target journal expects. Miller also sets no number of numbers per paragraph or section.

## Example

*An illustration of this file's procedure, not Miller's.* Draft sentence, in a Results section: "The model reproduced the observed peak within 5%, and the modelled peak at gauge D was 2.4013 m, an excellent result."

- **Context (rule 1):** which peak (which event, which quantity: water level or discharge)? "The observed peak" names no gauge or date.
- **Comparison (rule 4):** *within 5%* of what? Name the type of comparison (relative error of the modelled peak against the observed one) and its reference value.
- **Reference (rule 5):** *excellent* is judged against nothing stated. Ask the author what the agreement is judged against: the uncertainty of the observations, a criterion used in the field, or results of earlier models, with a source.
- **Digits (rule 6):** 2.4013 m implies a precision of 0.1 mm. Ask what precision the gauge and the model support.
- **Agreement (rule 7):** if Table 2 gives a value that differs beyond rounding, ask which is right. If it gives 2.40 m, the two agree once rounded, and only the digits in the text need attention (rule 6).
- **Revision:** keep the author's values and ask for the missing context, reference and precision; do not supply a criterion.

## Validation checklist

- [ ] The numbers the claims rest on were found; in a long draft, other numbers were checked only where a check found a problem
- [ ] Each of those numbers has a context (what, for which cases, when, where) that can be recovered (rule 1)
- [ ] Each value has its unit, and compared values share units and scale (rule 2)
- [ ] Each of those numbers is interpreted, with its direction and size (rule 3)
- [ ] Each comparison names its type and reference value, and suits the variable's level of measurement (rule 4)
- [ ] Each judgement of a value names what it is judged against, with a source (rule 5)
- [ ] Digits suit the measurement (rule 6)
- [ ] Values are plausible and agree with their tables and figures beyond rounding; any that do not were put to the author (rule 7)
- [ ] Statements of significance match the numbers, and statistical and substantive significance are kept apart (rule 8)
- [ ] Each statement of significance gives or points to its test result, and significance symbols are defined (rule 9)
- [ ] No value, unit, reference, standard or test result was invented

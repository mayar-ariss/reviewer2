# Discussions: Moreno and Swales's functional scheme

Load this file when the user is writing or revising the Discussion of an empirical research article, including a combined Results and Discussion or a Conclusion section that closes the article.

**Every rule below is Moreno and Swales's.** Each names its source by page. Where they give no rule, this file says so rather than supplying one. Nothing here is a general writing preference.

## Source and how it is cited

| Short form | Work |
|---|---|
| M&S 2018 (MS) | Moreno, A. I., & Swales, J. M. (2018). Strengthening move analysis methodology towards bridging the function-form gap. *English for Specific Purposes*, 50, 40–63. Cited from the authors' preprint; page numbers are the preprint's. |

The scheme comes from the ENEIDA project's analysis of 32 matched pairs of empirical research articles published in 2000–2010, one of each pair in an English-medium international journal and one in a Spanish-medium journal, across the life sciences, health sciences, social sciences and other natural sciences (pp. 15–16). It does not cover history (p. 28). Page numbers were read from the open preprint; check them against the published version before a release.

For the strength of the claims in a Discussion, apply `rules/claims.md` after this file.

## How to apply this file

*Procedure, not a rule of Moreno and Swales.* Their unit of analysis is the proposition, not the sentence (rule 2), so the agent works in this order:

1. **Ask for the field and the type of study**, or read them from the draft. Rule 10 depends on them.
2. **Label the draft.** Mark each proposition with its function: a step of one of the five moves, or an announcing or elaborating function (rules 3–4). Show the user the labelled draft.
3. **Check the draft against rules 5–10** and list what is missing, unsignalled or only implicit.
4. **Propose revisions citing the rule.** Keep the author's results, interpretations and citations. Do not invent explanations, comparisons, limitations or implications; where a step needs content the draft does not have, ask the author for it.
5. **Run the checklist** at the end.

## The scheme

Moreno and Swales's scheme for the Discussion and other closing sections (M&S 2018 (MS), Table 2, p. 27):

| Move | Steps |
|---|---|
| **Background information** | Re-stating key features of the current study; Reporting background information with citations; Providing background information without citations |
| **Summarizing or restating key results** | Presenting results neutrally; Contrasting with other results in the study; Highlighting results |
| **Commenting on key results or other features** | Establishing the meaning of results; Comparing with previous research; Explaining results or discussing effects; Making predictions; Reacting to results or other features |
| **Evaluating the current study or other research** | Pointing out negative features or limitations of the current study; Evaluating the state of knowledge or practice in broad terms; Stating the contribution of the current study; Pointing out positive features of the current study; Noting specific gaps in knowledge or deficiencies in other research or practice |
| **Drawing implications** | Making recommendations for future research or practice; Suggesting the applicability of results or usability of outcomes; Hypothesizing for future research |

Alongside the moves, two kinds of function serve the text without advancing it (p. 27):

- **Announcing:** announcing (sub)sections; announcing or referring the reader to external sources; announcing moves, steps or propositional meaning.
- **Elaborating:** justifying; exemplifying; clarifying.

## Rules

### 1. Treat the Discussion and any Conclusion as one closing section

*Source: M&S 2018 (MS), pp. 18, 26.*

Discussions often merge with the results or with the conclusions, so Moreno and Swales analyse the Discussion and any other closing section together as one functional block (pp. 18, 26).

- Apply the scheme to everything after the results, whatever the headings.
- In a combined Results and Discussion, label the propositions that report results too, with the steps of the summarizing move (rule 5).

### 2. Label propositions, not sentences

*Source: M&S 2018 (MS), pp. 19–21, 28, 39.*

The minimal unit is the proposition, so segmentation does not follow sentences (pp. 20, 39). A step is a fragment that adds new propositional meaning and whose function a competent reader can infer; it contains at least one verb, or a nominalization easily turned into one (pp. 20–21). Moves are functional, not formal, units (p. 19), and Moreno and Swales build them from steps, grouping steps by their overarching function (p. 28).

- One sentence may contain several steps; label each.
- A fragment with no new propositional meaning is not a step (see rule 3).

### 3. Separate announcing and elaborating from the moves

*Source: M&S 2018 (MS), pp. 23–24, 27.*

An announcing function points ahead: its meaning depends on text that follows, and it announces rather than expounds (pp. 23, 27). An elaborating function, such as justifying, exemplifying or clarifying, serves a neighbouring step without moving the text forward (pp. 23–24).

- Label these separately, and do not count them as moves.
- A Discussion made mostly of announcements and elaborations has little in it that advances the argument; point this out to the author.

### 4. Show what the study means beyond itself

*Source: M&S 2018 (MS), p. 11.*

Moreno and Swales describe Discussions as difficult because they must show significance beyond the study while balancing claims against their provisional nature; local research often fails to be accepted because its authors cannot project its wider value (p. 11).

- Check that the closing section does more than restate results: that it comments on them (rule 6), evaluates the study (rule 7) or draws implications (rule 8).
- Check that claims of wider value are stated with the caution their evidence supports (see `rules/claims.md`).

### 5. Restate results neutrally, by contrast, or with emphasis

*Source: M&S 2018 (MS), pp. 27, 30.*

The move of summarizing or restating key results has three steps: presenting results neutrally, contrasting them with other results in the study, and highlighting them (p. 27). Expressions such as *particularly important* signal highlighting (p. 30).

- Say which results the Discussion restates, and whether each restatement presents, contrasts or highlights.

### 6. Comment on the results: meaning, comparison, explanation, prediction, reaction

*Source: M&S 2018 (MS), pp. 6, 21, 27.*

The commenting move has five steps: establishing the meaning of results, comparing with previous research, explaining results or discussing effects, making predictions, and reacting to results or other features (p. 27). A comparison with previous research can be signalled by an opening such as *in accordance with prior research, we found* (p. 21); *because* and *since* signal explanation (p. 6, citing Lim 2010).

- For each key result, check whether the draft says what it means, how it compares with earlier work, and why it came out as it did.
- Where a comparison is made, check that the earlier work is cited.

### 7. Evaluate the study: limitations, contribution, strengths, gaps

*Source: M&S 2018 (MS), pp. 5, 27.*

The evaluating move has five steps: pointing out limitations of the current study, evaluating the state of knowledge or practice in broad terms, stating the study's contribution, pointing out its positive features, and noting specific gaps in knowledge or deficiencies in other research or practice (p. 27). *We* together with *lack* is reported as a signal of limitations (p. 5).

- Check that the draft states its contribution and its limitations; ask the author for either if it is missing.

### 8. Draw implications: recommendations, applications, hypotheses

*Source: M&S 2018 (MS), pp. 27, 31.*

The implications move has three steps: making recommendations for future research or practice, suggesting the applicability of results or usability of outcomes, and hypothesizing for future research (p. 27). A retrospective expression such as *another avenue for future research* can signal a hypothesis (p. 31).

- Check that any recommendation or application follows from the study's results.

### 9. Do not rely on surface signals alone

*Source: M&S 2018 (MS), pp. 5, 33.*

Moreno and Swales warn that forms can mislead: a lexical bundle may not signal the function it appears to (p. 5), and a word such as *concluded* can introduce a neutral presentation of results rather than an interpretation (p. 33).

- Label each proposition by what it does in context, as rule 2 requires, and use signals only as hints.

### 10. Fit the analysis to the type of study

*Source: M&S 2018 (MS), pp. 10–11, 16, 38.*

Moreno and Swales cite Williams (2011), who found the type of study to be the most influential factor in how discussions are structured, and they built their corpus to control for it (pp. 11, 16, 38). They also note that differences between earlier models may reflect the analysts' traditions more than the disciplines (p. 10).

- Compare the draft with recent Discussions in the target journal reporting the same type of study.

## Where Moreno and Swales give no rule

They do not specify which moves or steps are obligatory. The steps in their table are listed by likely frequency in English, but they make no claim about order, and positions may vary (p. 28). They give no quantitative results or teaching materials, which they defer to later papers (p. 8). Agreement between analysts was weak for two steps, suggesting applicability and reacting to results (pp. 34–36), so treat those labels with care. The scheme is a road map, which they say does not ensure publication (p. 11). This file adds nothing where they are silent.

## Example

*An illustration of this file's procedure, not Moreno and Swales's.* Draft Discussion, in full: "The model reproduced the observed peak within 5%. This confirms that the model is reliable. Future studies should use it."

- **Labels (rules 2, 5–8):** sentence 1 summarizes a key result (presenting results neutrally); sentence 2 comments on it (establishing the meaning of results); sentence 3 draws an implication (making recommendations for future research).
- **Missing moves (rule 7):** nothing evaluates the study: no limitation, contribution or strength. Ask the author what the study cannot show, and what it adds.
- **Comment (rule 6):** no comparison with previous research and no explanation. Ask whether earlier models of the event exist.
- **Implication (rule 8):** check that the recommendation follows from the results; a single reproduced peak may not support it (see `rules/argument.md` and `rules/claims.md`).

## Validation checklist

- [ ] Everything after the results was treated as one closing section (rule 1)
- [ ] Each proposition is labelled with a step, an announcing function or an elaborating function (rules 2–3)
- [ ] The section shows significance beyond the study, with appropriate caution (rule 4)
- [ ] Key results are restated, and each restatement presents, contrasts or highlights (rule 5)
- [ ] Key results are interpreted, compared with cited earlier work and explained (rule 6)
- [ ] The contribution and the limitations are stated (rule 7)
- [ ] Recommendations, applications and hypotheses follow from the results (rule 8)
- [ ] Labels rest on function in context, not on signal words alone (rule 9)
- [ ] The draft has been compared with Discussions of the same study type in the target journal (rule 10)
- [ ] No explanation, comparison, limitation or implication was invented; what the draft lacks was requested from the author

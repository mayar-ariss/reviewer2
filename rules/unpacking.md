# Unpacking: Halliday on grammatical metaphor

Load this file when the user wants a draft checked for sentences that are hard to read or that sound weighty but may say little: dense noun phrases, processes turned into nouns, and relations packed into a single verb such as *gives*, *leads to*, *shows* or *rests on*. It applies to any part of a research article, thesis, dissertation or proposal.

**Every rule below is Halliday's.** His books were not available to check, so rules cite them as reported in named open sources. Where he gives no rule, this file says so rather than supplying one. Nothing here is a general writing preference.

*Sources and how they are cited are listed at the end of this file.*

## How to apply this file

*Procedure, not a rule of Halliday.* Unpacking is used here as a test of what a sentence says, not as a style to rewrite into (rule 5):

1. **Mark the metaphorical wordings:** processes, qualities and relations expressed as nouns, and logical relations expressed as verbs (rules 1–2).
2. **Unpack each sentence that matters to the argument** into congruent wording: who or what does what, under what circumstances, linked by which conjunction (rules 3–4).
3. **Read the unpacked version.** If it has empty slots (no participant, no process, no stated relation), the original says less than it seems to; ask the author to fill them.
4. **Keep the metaphors that do work** (rule 5), and check density and ambiguity (rule 6).
5. **Run the checklist** at the end.

## Rules

### 1. Distinguish congruent from metaphorical wording

*Source: Halliday 1985, p. 321 (Randaccio, pp. 4, 13 n. 6).*

In Halliday's account a meaning has a typical, congruent realization in the grammar: processes as verbs, participants as nouns, logical relations as conjunctions. Grammatical metaphor realigns meaning and grammar, so that a meaning is expressed in another form, as when a process is reworded as a noun; a metaphorical expression has a congruent counterpart (Halliday 1985, p. 321, as reported by Randaccio, pp. 4, 13 n. 6). Randaccio's example: "The researcher set up the equipment carefully so the experiment succeeded" becomes "The researcher's careful setting up of the equipment resulted in experimental success" (p. 3).

- For a sentence that is hard to follow, ask what its congruent version would be.

### 2. Recognize nominalization as the main source of metaphor

*Source: Halliday, as quoted by Washitake; Randaccio, Table 2, p. 6.*

Halliday describes nominalizing as the most powerful resource for creating grammatical metaphor (as quoted by Washitake). The shifts include a process becoming an entity (*form* to *formation*), a quality becoming an entity (*present* to *presence*), a circumstance becoming a quality (*washed completely* to *complete washing*), and a logical relator becoming a process or entity (Randaccio, Table 2, p. 6).

- Mark nouns that stand for processes or qualities, and verbs that stand for logical relations.

### 3. Unpack a metaphorical sentence to see what it says

*Source: Washitake, applying Halliday; Randaccio, pp. 3, 8.*

Unpacking rewrites metaphorical wording into more congruent wording: "culture of cells in high glucose" becomes "cells are cultured in high glucose" (Washitake). Randaccio unpacks "theoretical molecular orbital calculation indicates almost no possibility of O2- formation" into a clause complex about what is possible according to how the orbitals are calculated (p. 8). Washitake recommends unpacking nominalizations into congruent clauses and checking the resulting meaning.

- Unpack the sentences that carry the argument. Report what the congruent version says, and what it is missing.

### 4. Find the two events and the relation in "A gives B" sentences

*Source: Halliday, as reported by Randaccio, p. 5; Washitake.*

The favourite clause type of modern scientific English, in Halliday's account, packs two events and the logical relation between them into one relational clause: a nominal group, a verb, a nominal group. Congruently, the same meaning is two clauses joined by a conjunction (Randaccio, p. 5). Randaccio's example: "higher calcination temperature gives higher catalytic activity", that is, when the calcination temperature is higher, the catalytic activity is higher (p. 5). Washitake recommends identifying logical relations hidden in verbs and nouns such as *resulting in*, *thereby* and *revealed*. Using Halliday's process types, Washitake distinguishes two kinds of "A shows B": verbal, where *show* introduces what is said ("we show that…"), and relational, where A is a sign of B ("the graph shows the result").

- For each *gives*, *leads to*, *causes*, *shows*, *reveals*, *rests on*, name the two events and the relation the verb stands for.
- If either event cannot be named, or the relation is unclear, ask the author.

### 5. Keep the metaphor that does scientific work

*Source: Randaccio, pp. 9–12; Washitake.*

Nominalization is not a fault in scientific writing. It creates technical terms and theoretical entities that can be classified and enter into further processes (Randaccio, pp. 9–10), and nominalized themes carry an argument forward from one clause to the next (pp. 11–12). Technical terms such as *ligation* are not unpacked (Washitake). Unpacked versions are clumsy and can change the meaning, and experts generally do not need them (Washitake).

- Do not propose unpacking as a rewrite where the metaphor is a technical term or carries the reasoning.
- Use unpacking to test a sentence; propose a congruent rewrite only where the metaphorical one hides who did what, or what relation is claimed.

### 6. Watch density and the ambiguity of nominalized clauses

*Source: Halliday 1993, pp. 76, 78 (Washitake).*

Halliday defines lexical density as a measure of the density of information in a passage (1993, p. 76, as reported by Washitake). He identifies the turning of clauses into nouns as the main cause of ambiguity in scientific English, because the relations a clause would state are lost (1993, p. 78, as reported by Washitake).

- Where a noun phrase could be unpacked in more than one way, flag it and ask the author which reading is meant.

### 7. Diagnose difficulty with Halliday's seven problems

*Source: Halliday 1993, pp. 69–85 (Washitake).*

Halliday argues that the difficulty of scientific English lies more in its grammar than in its vocabulary, and names seven sources of difficulty: interlocking definitions, technical taxonomies, special expressions, lexical density, syntactic ambiguity, grammatical metaphor and semantic discontinuity (1993, pp. 69–85, as reported by Washitake).

- When a passage is hard to read, name which of the seven is at work before proposing a change.

## Where Halliday gives no rule

Halliday describes how scientific English works and why it is hard; in the sources used here he does not tell writers to avoid nominalization or set limits on density. The use of unpacking as a test for empty claims (procedure step 3) is this skill's application of his distinction. Four of his seven problems (interlocking definitions, technical taxonomies, special expressions, semantic discontinuity) are listed without explanation in the reporting source, so this file gives no rules for them. This file adds nothing else where Halliday is silent.

## Example

*An illustration of this file's procedure, not Halliday's.* Draft sentence: "Recent crises show how much rests on their supply chains."

- **Metaphor (rules 2, 4):** *rests on* stands for a logical relation (depends on); *supply chains* stands for processes (how goods are sourced, made and moved); *show* may be relational (crises as a sign) or verbal (crises as a sayer).
- **Unpacked (rule 3):** "When [which] crises happen, [someone] can see that [what outcome] depends [to what degree] on how [which firms or countries] source and move goods."
- **Reading the unpacked version:** every slot that would make it a claim is empty: the crises, the observer or evidence, the outcome, the degree and the firms or countries. Ask the author to fill them; the sentence can then be written either way.

## Validation checklist

- [ ] Metaphorical wordings that carry the argument were marked (rules 1–2)
- [ ] Each was unpacked, and the unpacked version was read for empty slots (rule 3)
- [ ] Each relational verb was traced to two events and a stated relation (rule 4)
- [ ] Technical terms and argument-carrying nominalizations were left alone (rule 5)
- [ ] Ambiguous nominalized phrases were raised with the author (rule 6)
- [ ] Difficulty was diagnosed against the seven problems before changes were proposed (rule 7)
- [ ] No participant, process, relation or figure was invented

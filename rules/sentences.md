# Sentences: Gopen and Swan on reader expectations

Load this file when the user wants sentences or paragraphs checked for how they are built: whether each sentence tells the reader whose story it is, what happens, and what matters, in the places readers look for them. It applies to any part of a research article, thesis, dissertation or proposal, and can follow another rules file.

**Every rule below is Gopen and Swan's.** Each names its source by the section of their article. Where they give no rule, this file says so rather than supplying one. Nothing here is a general writing preference.

*Sources and how they are cited are listed at the end of this file.*

## How to apply this file

*Procedure, not a rule of Gopen and Swan.* Their method starts from what readers expect to find where (rule 1), so the agent works in this order:

1. **For each sentence, find the three positions:** the topic position (the opening, up to the subject), the verb, and the stress position (the end of the sentence or of a clause closed by a colon or semicolon).
2. **Ask of each sentence:** Whose story is it, and is that in the topic position (rule 3)? What is the action, and is it in the verb (rule 6)? What is new and important, and is it in the stress position (rule 5)?
3. **Check the links between sentences** (rules 4, 7, 9).
4. **Propose revisions citing the rule.** Keep the author's content. Do not invent the actions, agents, links or findings a sentence lacks; where a revision needs them, ask the author.
5. **Run the checklist** at the end.

## The principles

Gopen and Swan's structural principles, which they present as principles rather than rules (G&S 1990, "Writing and the Scientific Process"):

1. Follow a grammatical subject as soon as possible with its verb.
2. Place in the stress position the "new information" you want the reader to emphasize.
3. Place the person or thing whose "story" a sentence is telling at the beginning of the sentence, in the topic position.
4. Place appropriate "old information" (material already stated in the discourse) in the topic position for linkage backward and contextualization forward.
5. Articulate the action of every clause or sentence in its verb.
6. In general, provide context for your reader before asking that reader to consider anything new.
7. In general, try to ensure that the relative emphases of the substance coincide with the relative expectations for emphasis raised by the structure.

## Rules

### 1. Put information where readers expect it

*Source: G&S 1990, "Writing with the Reader in Mind: Expectation and Context"; "Reader Expectations for the Structure of Prose".*

Readers do not simply read; they interpret, and they make many decisions about substance from clues in structure. Information is interpreted more easily and more uniformly when it is placed where most readers expect to find it; when expectations are violated, effort goes into the structure instead of the content. A unit of discourse, of whatever size, should serve a single function or make a single point.

- Judge each sentence by what a reader would take from its structure, not by what the writer meant.

### 2. Keep the verb close to its subject

*Source: G&S 1990, "Subject-Verb Separation"; principle 1.*

Readers expect the verb to follow the subject at once. Material between them reads as an interruption and is treated as less important, even when it is not. Gopen and Swan recommend moving such material to a position that shows its importance, or deleting it if it is only an aside.

- Mark long stretches between a subject and its verb, and say whether the material should move or go.

### 3. Open with whose story the sentence tells

*Source: G&S 1990, "The Topic Position"; principle 3.*

The topic position sets the perspective: readers expect a sentence to be about whoever or whatever comes first. Gopen and Swan show this by comparing active and passive versions of the same fact, which tell different stories.

- For each sentence, ask whose story it is, and check that this is what opens the sentence.
- Where the opening names something the sentence is not really about, flag the mismatch.

### 4. Link back with old information in the topic position

*Source: G&S 1990, "The Topic Position"; principle 4.*

The topic position should carry a link to what came before, so that the reader has context for what follows. Opening sentence after sentence with new information makes the reader lose track of the story. Gopen and Swan add that this is a principle, not a formula: the middle of a sentence also holds old and new information.

- Check that each sentence's opening connects to the sentence before it.

### 5. End with the new information you want stressed

*Source: G&S 1990, "The Stress Position"; principle 2.*

Readers naturally emphasize the end of a sentence, the point of syntactic closure, so the stress position should hold the material that most deserves emphasis. A colon or semicolon creates a secondary stress position. Misuse has two results: the stress position holds material that does not deserve emphasis, or it stresses something the writer did not mean to stress; either way the reader may misread the emphasis.

- Read the last words of each sentence: are they the new information the writer wants the reader to take away?
- Where the stress position holds something vague or minor, ask the author what the point is.

### 6. Put the action in the verb

*Source: G&S 1990, "Locating the Action"; principle 5.*

Readers expect the action of a sentence to be stated by its verb. When the verbs are weak forms such as *is* or *are presumed to be*, readers have to guess what is happening. When the action is buried in nouns or left out, each reader makes a personal guess, and the writer loses control of the interpretation.

- For each clause, name the action. If the main verb does not state it, find where it is hidden, or note that it is missing.
- Do not supply a missing action yourself; ask the author what happens.

### 7. Give context before asking the reader to take in anything new

*Source: G&S 1990, "Writing and the Scientific Process"; principle 6.*

Gopen and Swan's sixth principle asks the writer, in general, to provide context before asking the reader to consider anything new.

- Check that new terms, results and claims arrive after what the reader needs to place them.

### 8. Make structural emphasis match the importance of the content

*Source: G&S 1990, "Reader Expectations for the Structure of Prose"; "The Stress Position"; principle 7.*

Length alone does not make a sentence hard. A sentence is too long when it has more candidates for emphasis than it has stress positions to hold them. The relative emphasis the structure creates should match the relative importance of the content.

- Where a sentence has several points competing for emphasis, propose splitting it or adding a secondary stress position.

### 9. State the logical links; do not leave them for the reader

*Source: G&S 1990, "Perceiving Logical Gaps"; "Writing and the Scientific Process".*

When the connections between statements are missing, readers have to supply the logic themselves. Writers often omit these links because they seem obvious to them. In Gopen and Swan's revisions, attending to structure exposed gaps in the science, not only in the prose: the structure of the prose becomes the structure of the scientific argument.

- Where one sentence follows another without a stated connection, ask how they connect.
- Treat a gap the author cannot fill as a question about the argument, not about wording.

### 10. Break a principle only for effect

*Source: G&S 1990, "Writing and the Scientific Process".*

The principles are not rules, and any reader expectation can be violated to good effect. Skilled writers fulfil expectations most of the time, so that a deliberate violation stands out.

- Before flagging a violation, consider whether it is deliberate and effective. Flag it if it is neither.

## Where Gopen and Swan give no rule

The article does not discuss pronouns or reference, such as what *this*, *it* or *their* points to; for that, see `rules/cohesion.md`. It gives no limit on sentence length in words (rule 8), no fixed algorithm for good writing, and no rule for vague quantities (see `rules/vagueness.md`). Its principles are principles, not rules (rule 10). This file adds nothing where Gopen and Swan are silent.

## Example

*An illustration of this file's procedure, not Gopen and Swan's.* Draft sentence: "Extreme events show how much rests on their hydraulics."

- **Whose story (rule 3):** the topic position holds *extreme events*, but the sentence seems to be about the hydraulics of something else: structures, channels or rivers. Ask the author whose story it is.
- **Action (rule 6):** the main verb *show* states no action in the system being described. The real action, something depending on hydraulics, is hidden in *rests on*, inside a clause, and never stated as a relation between named things.
- **Stress position (rule 5):** the sentence ends on *their hydraulics*, which is not new information the reader can take away; what matters, and how much, is left unsaid.
- **Logical gap (rule 9):** the link between the events and the hydraulics is asserted, not stated.
- **Revision:** ask the author for the structure, the hydraulic property, the outcome and the evidence, then build the sentence so that whose story it is opens it, the action is the verb and the outcome is the stress position: "During [event], [structure]'s [hydraulic property] determined whether [outcome] ([citation])."
- **Not covered here:** *their* refers to events, which have no hydraulics (see `rules/cohesion.md`); *how much* states no quantity (see `rules/vagueness.md`); the missing source and the unearned certainty of *show* are for `rules/claims.md`; unpacking the sentence to show its empty slots is in `rules/unpacking.md`.

## Validation checklist

- [ ] Each sentence was read for what its structure tells the reader (rule 1)
- [ ] Subjects are followed closely by their verbs (rule 2)
- [ ] Each sentence opens with whose story it tells (rule 3)
- [ ] Each opening links back to what came before (rule 4)
- [ ] Each stress position holds the new information the writer wants stressed (rule 5)
- [ ] The action of each clause is stated in its verb (rule 6)
- [ ] Context comes before new material (rule 7)
- [ ] Structural emphasis matches the importance of the content (rule 8)
- [ ] Logical links between sentences are stated (rule 9)
- [ ] Violations of the principles are deliberate and effective, or flagged (rule 10)
- [ ] No action, agent, link or finding was invented; what the draft lacks was requested from the author

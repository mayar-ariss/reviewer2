# Abstracts: Hyland's five moves

Load this file when the user is writing or revising the abstract of a research article, thesis or dissertation.

**Every rule below is Hyland's.** His book was not available to check, so rules cite it as reported in a named open source. Where he gives no rule, this file says so rather than supplying one. Nothing here is a general writing preference.

## Sources and how they are cited

| Short form | Work |
|---|---|
| Hyland 2000 | Hyland, K. (2000). *Disciplinary Discourses: Social Interactions in Academic Writing*. Harlow: Longman. Chapter on abstracts. |

Each rule cites Hyland 2000 as reported by:

| Reporting source | Work |
|---|---|
| S&U | Suntara, W., & Usaha, S. (2013). Research article abstracts in two related disciplines: Rhetorical variation between linguistics and applied linguistics. *English Language Teaching*, 6(2), 84–99. Open access: https://files.eric.ed.gov/fulltext/EJ1076961.pdf |

Hyland's model was derived from 800 abstracts, 100 in each of eight disciplines: philosophy, sociology, applied linguistics, marketing, electrical engineering, mechanical engineering, physics and biology (S&U). It covers both the sciences and the social sciences (S&U).

## How to apply this file

*Procedure, not a rule of Hyland's.*

1. **Ask for the field and the target journal**, and whether the journal requires a structured abstract or a word limit.
2. **Label the abstract.** Mark each sentence with its move (see the model). Show the user the labelled abstract.
3. **Check the moves against rules 1–4** and list what is missing or out of proportion.
4. **Check the abstract against the paper**, sentence by sentence (rule 5).
5. **Propose revisions citing the rule.** Keep the author's findings and claims. Do not invent results, methods or implications; where a move needs content the abstract does not have, take it from the paper or ask the author.
6. **Run the checklist** at the end.

## The model

Hyland's classification of moves in research article abstracts (Hyland 2000, p. 67, as reproduced by S&U):

| Move | Function |
|---|---|
| **Introduction** | Establishes the context of the paper and motivates the research or discussion |
| **Purpose** | Indicates the purpose, thesis or hypothesis; outlines the intention behind the paper |
| **Method** | Gives information on design, procedures, assumptions, approach, data |
| **Product** | States the main findings or results, the argument, or what was accomplished |
| **Conclusion** | Interprets or extends the results beyond the scope of the paper, draws inferences, points to applications or wider implications |

## Rules

### 1. Account for each of the five moves

*Source: Hyland 2000, p. 67 (S&U).*

- For each move, say whether the abstract realizes it, and in which sentence.
- Where a move is missing, ask whether the field and the journal expect it (rule 4).

### 2. State the purpose as its own move

*Source: Hyland 2000, p. 67 (S&U).*

Hyland separates the Purpose move, which states the purpose, thesis or hypothesis, from the Introduction move, which sets the context.

- Check that the abstract states what the paper sets out to do, not only why the topic matters.

### 3. State the product: what was found or accomplished

*Source: Hyland 2000, p. 67 (S&U).*

The Product move states the main findings or results, the argument, or what was accomplished.

- Check that the abstract reports the main findings themselves, not only that findings exist ("results are discussed").

### 4. Give context and the problem in proportion to the field

*Source: Hyland 2000 (S&U).*

Hyland saw identifying a problem as a critical step in all disciplines, and a crucial one in the soft fields, where abstracts give more context (S&U, reporting Hyland 2000).

- In the humanities and social sciences, check that the abstract frames the problem the paper addresses.
- In any field, check that the Introduction move leads to a problem, not only to the importance of the topic.

### 5. Keep the conclusion within the paper's results

*Source: Hyland 2000, p. 67 (S&U). Application to checking is this skill's.*

The Conclusion move interprets or extends results beyond the scope of the paper and points to wider implications. Because it reaches beyond the paper, it is the move most likely to claim more than the results show.

- Check each sentence of the Conclusion move against the paper's results, and, in health research, apply `rules/spin.md` to it.
- Check that every finding in the Product move appears in the paper's Results.

## Where Hyland gives no rule

The source used here does not report which moves Hyland found obligatory or optional, their usual order, the verb forms used in each, or any rule on length. Journal requirements, such as structured headings and word limits, take precedence and are not covered here. Swales and Feak's *Abstracts and the Writing of Abstracts* (2009) was not used. This file adds nothing where Hyland is silent.

## Example

*An illustration of this file's procedure, not Hyland's.* Draft abstract: "Flood risk is a growing concern worldwide. This paper examines hydraulic modelling of extreme events. Results are presented and their implications discussed."

- **Moves (rules 1–3):** Introduction (sentence 1), a partial Purpose (sentence 2), and no Product: "results are presented" states no finding. There is no Method move.
- **Problem (rule 4):** the Introduction gives the importance of the topic but no problem.
- **Revision:** ask the author for the problem, the method, the main findings and the conclusion, taking them from the paper where they are stated there.

## Validation checklist

- [ ] Each sentence is labelled with its move (rule 1)
- [ ] The purpose is stated, separately from the context (rule 2)
- [ ] The main findings are stated, not just announced (rule 3)
- [ ] The problem is framed in proportion to the field (rule 4)
- [ ] The conclusion and every reported finding are supported by the paper (rule 5)
- [ ] Journal requirements on structure and length were checked
- [ ] No result, method or implication was invented

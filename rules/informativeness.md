# Informativeness: Grice's maxims

Load this file when the user wants a draft checked for sentences that tell the reader nothing new: truisms, platitudes and general principles that any reader of the field already accepts ("What a simulation can say depends on how real its inputs are"), sentences whose point is unclear, and loose words that hide what is meant (inputs that are "real"). It applies to any part of a research article, thesis, dissertation or proposal.

**Every rule below is Grice's.** His essay was not available to check, so rules cite it as reported in named open sources. Grice wrote about conversation, not academic writing; where a rule applies his maxims to a research text, the file says so. Where he gives no rule, this file says so rather than supplying one. Nothing here is a general writing preference.

*Sources and how they are cited are listed at the end of this file.*

## How to apply this file

*Procedure, not a rule of Grice.*

1. **For each sentence, say in one line what it tells the reader** that the reader did not know or accept before (rule 2).
2. **Flag sentences that tell the reader nothing new** at the point where they stand: truisms, tautologies, principles every reader of the field already holds (rules 2–3).
3. **For each flagged sentence, ask what the author meant to imply,** and whether the paper has the evidence to say it outright (rules 3–4).
4. **Check that each sentence serves the paper's purpose and is clear** (rules 5–7).
5. **Propose changes citing the rule.** Do not replace a truism with a specific claim the author has not made; ask the author for the specific claim and its evidence.
6. **Run the checklist** at the end.

## The maxims

Grice's Cooperative Principle asks a speaker to make each contribution such as is required, at the stage at which it occurs, by the accepted purpose or direction of the exchange (Grice 1989, p. 26, as quoted in SEP Grice). He groups more specific maxims under four categories (Grice, pp. 26–30, as cited in SEP Implicature):

| Category | Maxim |
|---|---|
| **Quantity** | Make your contribution as informative as is required; do not make it more informative than is required (SEP Grice). |
| **Quality** | Try to make your contribution one that is true: do not say what you believe false, or what you lack adequate evidence for (SEP Grice; SEP Implicature). |
| **Relation** | Be relevant (SEP Grice; SEP Implicature). |
| **Manner** | Be perspicuous: avoid obscurity and ambiguity, and be brief and orderly (SEP Implicature). |

## Rules

### 1. Read each sentence against the purpose of the paper at that point

*Source: Grice 1989, p. 26 (SEP Grice). Application to research writing is this skill's.*

The Cooperative Principle measures a contribution against what is required, at the stage it occurs, by the purpose of the exchange (p. 26, as quoted in SEP Grice). In a research text the purpose at a given point is set by the section and its argument.

- Before judging a sentence, say what the paragraph it sits in is trying to establish.

### 2. Make each sentence as informative as the point requires

*Source: Grice, Quantity (SEP Grice; SEP Implicature).*

Quantity asks for a contribution as informative as is required, and no more (SEP Grice).

- Flag sentences that leave the reader with nothing they did not already know or accept.
- Flag the opposite too: detail that the point does not require.

### 3. Treat a truism as a sign of an unstated point

*Source: Grice, on flouting (p. 34, SEP Implicature); on tautologies (Sider). Application to research writing is this skill's.*

Grice's example "War is war" is a triviality, and so, taken literally, not worth saying (Sider). When what a cooperative speaker says patently violates a maxim, the hearer infers that the speaker means something different (Grice, p. 34, as quoted in SEP Implicature). A reader who meets a truism in a research text will either skip it or look for what it implies.

- For each truism, ask the author what it is meant to imply here: a limitation of the present study, a reason for a design choice, a gap in earlier work?
- Propose stating that point outright, in the author's words, in place of the truism.

### 4. Do not cure an uninformative sentence with an unsupported one

*Source: Grice, Quality; the conflict between Quantity and Quality (SEP Grice; SEP Implicature).*

Quality asks the speaker not to say what they lack adequate evidence for (SEP Implicature). The maxims can conflict: a speaker may be caught between saying something less informative than is desired, which violates Quantity, and saying something for which there is insufficient evidence, which violates Quality (SEP Grice).

- Where the specific claim behind a truism would need evidence the draft does not have, ask the author for it; if it does not exist, say so (see `rules/claims.md` for calibrating the claim).
- Never supply a figure, result or citation to make a sentence informative.

### 5. Keep each sentence relevant to the argument

*Source: Grice, Relation (SEP Grice; SEP Implicature).*

Relation asks the speaker to be relevant (SEP Grice).

- Flag sentences that do not serve the point of their paragraph, however true they are.

### 6. Be clear: avoid obscurity and ambiguity, be brief and orderly

*Source: Grice, Manner (SEP Implicature).*

Manner asks the speaker to be perspicuous: to avoid obscurity and ambiguity, and to strive for brevity and order (SEP Implicature).

- Flag sentences a reader could take in more than one way, or whose point is hidden.
- Flag words and phrases that add nothing to the claim, and propose the shorter wording.

### 7. Name the property, the case and the output that are meant

*Source: Grice, Manner (SEP Implicature). Application to word choice in research writing is this skill's.*

Manner asks the speaker to avoid obscurity (SEP Implicature). In a research text, an everyday word standing in for a technical property, or a general class standing in for a particular case, leaves the reader to guess what is meant.

- Where a word names a loose property (*real*, *good*, *important*, *significant* used non-statistically), ask the author which definite property is meant, and offer the candidates. Do not choose the term yourself where several could be meant.
- Where the subject is a general class (*a simulation*, *studies*, *models*), ask whether a particular one is meant.
- Where an abstract phrase stands for something concrete (*what a simulation can say*), ask which outputs or conclusions are meant.
- Vagueness that suits its purpose is not obscurity: for vague quantities, see `rules/vagueness.md`.

## Where Grice gives no rule

Grice describes conversation; he gives no rules for academic writing, says nothing about technical terminology, and no measure of how informative a contribution must be apart from the purpose of the exchange (rule 1). Applying the maxims to a research text, the advice in rules 3 and 4 on what to do with a truism, and the word-level checks in rule 7 are this skill's applications. Because the essay itself was not available, this file covers only the points the reporting sources give. This file adds nothing else where Grice is silent.

## Example

*An illustration of this file's procedure, not Grice's.* Draft sentence, from a political science paper: "What a survey can tell us depends on how honest its answers are."

- **What it tells the reader (rule 2):** that survey findings are only as good as the responses behind them, which any reader of survey research already accepts. At the level of what is said, it is close to a tautology.
- **What it implies (rule 3):** ask the author. Is this the authors' own survey? Which estimates are limited by which answers? Is this a limitation of the present study?
- **Obscure wording (rule 7):** answers are not more or less *honest* as a measured property. Ask which property is meant: the accuracy of self-reports, social desirability bias, the reliability of the measure, or how representative the respondents are. *A survey* names no particular survey: ask whether it is the authors' own. *What a survey can tell us* stands for particular estimates: ask which.
- **Evidence (rule 4):** a specific version ("reported turnout exceeds validated turnout in our sample") needs results or a citation. Ask for them; do not supply them.

## Validation checklist

- [ ] Each sentence was read against the purpose of its paragraph (rule 1)
- [ ] Sentences that tell the reader nothing new were flagged (rule 2)
- [ ] For each truism, the author was asked what it is meant to imply (rule 3)
- [ ] No uninformative sentence was replaced by an unsupported one (rule 4)
- [ ] Irrelevant sentences were flagged (rule 5)
- [ ] Obscure or ambiguous sentences, and words that add nothing, were flagged (rule 6)
- [ ] Loose words for properties, general classes and abstract phrases were flagged, with candidate terms offered and the choice left to the author (rule 7)
- [ ] No claim, figure, result or citation was invented

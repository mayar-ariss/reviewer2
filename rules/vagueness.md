# Vague quantities: Channell on vague language

Load this file when the user wants a draft checked for vague quantities and categories: approximations (*about 40*, *40 or so*), quantities with no number (*many*, *a lot of*, *how much*, *most*), vague categories (*and so on*, *and things like that*) and words that stand in for a missing term. It applies to any part of a research article, thesis, dissertation or proposal.

**Every rule below is Channell's.** Her book was not available to check in full, so rules cite its sections and pages from the published table of contents, or quotations of it in named open sources. Where she gives no rule, this file says so rather than supplying one. Nothing here is a general writing preference.

## How to apply this file

*Procedure, not a rule of Channell.*

1. **Mark every vague expression of quantity or category** (rule 2).
2. **For each, ask what it does** (rule 3): does it give the right amount of information, or does it withhold, persuade, cover a gap in knowledge or protect the writer?
3. **Decide whether it is appropriate** in a research text at that point (rules 1, 4).
4. **Propose changes citing the rule.** Do not supply figures; where a quantity should be stated, ask the author for it.
5. **Run the checklist** at the end.

## Rules

### 1. Judge vagueness by whether it is appropriate, not by whether it is vague

*Source: Channell 1994, pp. 3–4 (ISSA; Ondari & Michieka).*

For Channell, vagueness in language is neither all bad nor all good: what matters is that vague language is used appropriately (p. 3, as quoted by ISSA and by Ondari & Michieka). Speakers and writers vary their level of precision to suit the situation (p. 4, as reported by ISSA).

- Do not flag every approximation or quantifier. Flag those that are wrong for their purpose.

### 2. Recognize the forms vague quantities take

*Source: Channell 1994, chs. 3–7 (table of contents).*

Channell treats these forms, each in its own chapter:

| Form | Examples | Channell 1994 |
|---|---|---|
| Numbers with approximators | *about*, *around*, *approximately*, *n or m*, *n or so* | ch. 3, pp. 42–75 |
| Round numbers used approximately, and exaggeration | *a hundred*, *thousands of* | ch. 4, pp. 78–94 |
| Non-numerical vague quantifiers | *a lot of*, *loads of*, *many*, *some*, adverbs of frequency | ch. 5, pp. 95–118 |
| Vague category identifiers | *and things like that*, *or something* | ch. 6, pp. 119–143 |
| Placeholder words | *thingy*, *whatsisname* | ch. 7, pp. 157–164 |

She also asks how a reader can tell when an apparently exact number is an approximation (ch. 4, p. 87).

- Include round numbers and frequency adverbs (*often*, *usually*) in the check, not only *many* and *about*.

### 3. Identify what each vague expression is doing

*Source: Channell 1994, ch. 8, pp. 165–194 (table of contents); pp. 170, 179 (ISSA).*

Channell's chapter on the uses of vague language distinguishes these functions (ch. 8):

- giving the right amount of information (p. 173);
- deliberately withholding information (p. 178);
- using language persuasively (p. 179);
- lexical gaps, when there is no precise word available (p. 180);
- lacking specific information (p. 184);
- displacement (p. 186);
- self-protection (p. 188);
- power and politeness (p. 190);
- informality and atmosphere (p. 191).

Vague language lets speakers talk about a topic they know little about, or lack the vocabulary for (p. 170, as reported by ISSA). Withholding information the hearer expects violates the maxim of quantity and gives rise to implicatures (p. 179, as quoted by ISSA).

- Name the function of each vague expression before judging it.

### 4. In a research text, state the quantity where the vagueness withholds, persuades or hides a gap

*Source: Channell 1994, pp. 173, 178–179, 184 (table of contents; ISSA). Application to research writing is this skill's.*

Channell's functions sort vague quantities in a draft into those that suit their purpose and those that do not:

- **Right amount of information (p. 173):** an approximation is enough for the point being made. Leave it.
- **Withholding (p. 178):** the writer has the figure but does not give it, and the reader draws an implicature from its absence (p. 179, ISSA). Ask the author for the figure.
- **Persuasion (p. 179):** the vagueness makes the claim sound larger than it is shown to be. Ask for the figure, or for wording the evidence supports.
- **Lacking specific information (p. 184):** the writer does not have the figure. Say so explicitly, and calibrate the claim (see `rules/claims.md`).

## Where Channell gives no rule

Channell describes how vague language works, mostly in speech; she does not set rules for academic writing in the sources used here. The judgement in rule 4 of which functions suit a research text is this skill's application of her categories, not her prescription. Her 1990 study of quantities in economics writing would be the place to check that application. This file adds nothing else where Channell is silent.

## Example

*An illustration of this file's procedure, not Channell's.* Draft sentence: "Extreme events show how much rests on their hydraulics."

- **Form (rule 2):** *how much* is a non-numerical vague quantity, with no number, range or unit.
- **Function (rules 3–4):** it does not give the right amount of information, since the reader cannot tell whether a little or a great deal rests on the hydraulics. It reads as persuasion (the quantity sounds large) or as a cover for missing information.
- **Revision:** ask the author whether a measure exists (a discharge, a failure rate, a cost) and state it; if none exists, say so and calibrate the claim with `rules/claims.md`.

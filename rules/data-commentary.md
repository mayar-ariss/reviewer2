# Data commentary: Swales and Feak on writing about figures and tables

Load this file when the user wants the text that accompanies a figure or table checked: what it says about the visual, which results it singles out, how it interprets them, and how strongly. It applies wherever a draft comments on data, usually in the Results and Discussion.

**Every rule below is Swales and Feak's.** Rules cite their 1995 article by page, and their textbook as reported in a named open source. Where they give no rule, this file says so rather than supplying one. Nothing here is a general writing preference, and nothing here concerns the design of the visual itself.

## How to apply this file

*Procedure, not a rule of Swales and Feak's.*

1. **For each table and figure, collect the text that comments on it**, and ask what the paper's question or hypothesis is at that point.
2. **Count the figures and tables**, and compare the number with recent articles in the target journal and with its author guidelines. The sources give no rule on how many a paper should have, so report the comparison and leave the decision to the author. Whether each figure is needed at all is checked in `rules/argument.md` (rule 6) and `rules/results.md` (rule 1).
3. **Label each sentence** with its element: location or summary, highlighting, or discussion (see the structure).
4. **Check the commentary against rules 1–8** (rule 8 for graphs).
5. **Propose revisions citing the rule.** Do not supply explanations, comparisons or critiques of method; where the commentary needs them, ask the author.
6. **Run the checklist** at the end.

## The structure

Swales and Feak describe data commentaries as usually having these elements, in this order (AWG, as reported by T&R, p. 11):

| Element | What it does |
|---|---|
| **Location elements and summary statements** | Tell the reader where the data are and what they show overall |
| **Highlighting statements** | Single out the results that matter |
| **Discussion** | Takes up implications, problems, exceptions or recommendations |

## Rules

### 1. Write a commentary, not a restatement of the visual

*Source: S&F 1995, pp. 79, 82, 85–86.*

Writing that accompanies non-verbal material typically takes the form of a selective and interpretive commentary; the writer must decide what, if anything, to repeat from the visual and how to interpret the data (p. 79). Swales and Feak ask why one would say the same thing twice, once in words and once in non-verbal form (p. 82). Commentaries that simply repeat the contents of a table they treat as information transfer, not commentary (pp. 85–86).

- Flag sentences that restate values from a table or figure without singling them out or interpreting them, and ask what the reader should take from them.
- Flag the same data shown twice, in a table and a figure, or in a figure and in full in the text, and ask the author which presentation to keep. *Extending Swales and Feak's point from text and visual to two visuals is this skill's application.*

### 2. Locate the visual where the reader needs it

*Source: S&F 1995, p. 82; AWG (T&R, p. 11).*

Among the writer's decisions is where, if at all, to refer the reader to the location of the non-verbal content (p. 82). Location elements and summary statements open the commentary (AWG, as reported by T&R).

- Check that the commentary tells the reader which visual it discusses before it discusses the results, and, where useful, what the visual shows overall.
- For a graph, check that the reader is told what the axes represent and what any shading or symbols indicate, in the text or the caption (AWG 1994, as adapted by Yoo).

### 3. Highlight the results that bear on the question, and say why

*Source: S&F 1995, pp. 82, 85; AWG (T&R, p. 11).*

The writer must decide which results to highlight, and for what reasons (p. 82). In Swales and Feak's analysis, a weak commentary highlighted aspects of the data that were not the most relevant to the purpose of the research, showing difficulty in distinguishing the more important facts from the less important (p. 85).

- For each highlighted result, ask how it bears on the question or hypothesis. Flag results that are highlighted but irrelevant to it, and results that matter to it but are not mentioned.

### 4. Account for results that do not fit, and link them to the question

*Source: S&F 1995, pp. 85–87, 91.*

Swales and Feak criticize commentaries that note an exception, or compare it with the other data, without explaining it or saying how it bears on the hypothesis (pp. 85–86). The stronger commentary noted the exception, interpreted it and linked it to the hypothesis, constructing something that could not be readily seen in the table (p. 87). Where data show differences, a reader may reasonably expect some explanation of them (p. 91).

- For each result that departs from the pattern or the prediction, check that the commentary says what it means for the question. Ask the author for the explanation; do not supply one.

### 5. Qualify interpretations to fit the data

*Source: S&F 1995, pp. 82, 87, 89.*

The writer must decide what conclusions or implications can be drawn from the data, and how they should be qualified or hedged (p. 82). The successful commentaries in Swales and Feak's analysis showed awareness that claims must be stated carefully yet confidently (pp. 87, 89).

- Check that each interpretation is no stronger than the data shown (see `rules/claims.md` for hedges and boosters).

### 6. Decide whether to go beyond the data: context, method, other studies

*Source: S&F 1995, pp. 82, 88–89.*

The writer may need to bring in a broader context, critique the methodology, or compare the results with others (p. 82). In Swales and Feak's analysis, a commentary that critiqued the method behind the data was a successful but high-risk strategy, while one that interpreted the data within the study was successful and safe (pp. 88–89).

- Where the commentary goes beyond the data, check that the context, critique or comparison is supported and cited. Where it does not, ask whether the field expects it.

### 7. Connect the statements into one commentary

*Source: S&F 1995, p. 86.*

In Swales and Feak's analysis, what most weakened one commentary was the lack of explicit connections among its statements (p. 86).

- Check that the highlighting statements and the discussion are linked into one account, not listed (see `rules/sentences.md`, rule 9, and `rules/cohesion.md`).

### 8. For a graph, highlight the pattern and say what it suggests

*Source: S&F 1995, p. 79; AWG 1994, Unit 4 (Yoo).*

The non-verbal material Swales and Feak have in mind includes graphs and charts as well as tables (p. 79). In AWG's account as adapted by Yoo, highlighting moves from the details to the overall pattern; graphs are described in terms of trends (increases and decreases, levelling off, fluctuation, high and low points, sharp or gradual change); and the commentary can close by offering possible causes of the results and saying what a trend suggests, including its implications (Yoo).

- Flag graph commentary that reads off individual values without stating the pattern they form, and ask the author for the trend and its turning points.
- Where the commentary explains a trend or projects it forward, apply rules 4 and 5: the explanation is the author's, and the claim is no stronger than the data.

## Where Swales and Feak give no rule

The sources used here give no rule on the content of captions and legends, on which results deserve a visual at all, on how many figures and tables a paper should have, or on the design of tables and figures; the checks on duplication (rule 1) and on number (procedure step 2) are this skill's. Only pages 78–91 of the 1995 article were available, and the textbook was not read directly; AWG's structure is cited as reported by a scoping review, and its guidance on graphs as adapted in a course handout, which does not separate Swales and Feak's wording from the adapter's. This file adds nothing where Swales and Feak are silent.

## Example

*An illustration of this file's procedure, not Swales and Feak's.* Draft commentary on a table of observed and modelled peak water levels at four gauges: "Table 2 shows the results. At gauge A the observed level was 3.21 m and the modelled level 3.18 m; at gauge B, 2.95 m and 2.90 m; at gauge C, 2.40 m and 2.37 m; at gauge D, 1.80 m and 2.40 m."

- **Elements:** a location element, then restated values; no highlighting statement and no discussion.
- **Restatement (rule 1):** every value is repeated from the table. Ask what the reader should take from them.
- **Highlighting (rule 3):** the result that bears on the model's accuracy, the large overestimate at gauge D, is not singled out.
- **Exception (rule 4):** gauge D departs from the pattern. Ask the author what explains it and what it means for the model's use.
- **Strength (rule 5):** once the author interprets the agreement at A–C, check that the claim fits four gauges and one event.

A second draft, on a figure of observed and modelled discharge over the flood: "Figure 3 shows the hydrographs. At 06:00 the observed discharge was 120 m³/s and the modelled 115 m³/s; at 12:00, 410 and 455 m³/s; at 18:00, 300 and 290 m³/s."

- **Orientation (rule 2):** the text does not say what the axes or the two lines are; check that the caption does.
- **Pattern (rule 8):** the commentary reads off three points. Ask for the pattern: both hydrographs rise to a peak and recede, and the model overestimates the peak.
- **Exception and meaning (rules 4, 8):** ask the author whether the peak is also early or late, what explains the overestimate, and what it means for the model's use; check that the claim fits one event.

## Validation checklist

- [ ] Each sentence of the commentary is labelled with its element
- [ ] Values are not restated without being singled out or interpreted, and no data are shown twice (rule 1)
- [ ] The number of figures and tables was compared with the target journal (procedure)
- [ ] The reader is told which visual is discussed, and where useful what it shows (rule 2)
- [ ] Highlighted results bear on the question; relevant results are not left out (rule 3)
- [ ] Results that do not fit are interpreted and linked to the question (rule 4)
- [ ] Interpretations are no stronger than the data (rule 5)
- [ ] Any context, critique or comparison is supported (rule 6)
- [ ] The statements form one connected commentary (rule 7)
- [ ] For a graph, the axes and symbols are explained, and the commentary states the pattern rather than reading off points (rules 2, 8)
- [ ] No explanation, comparison or critique was invented
